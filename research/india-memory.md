# Public memory timeline: 40 feminist milestones, India first

Research date: 2026-10-24. This is a verified "public memory" timeline for the Cultural Shock Party (CSP) website. It has 28 Indian milestones and 12 global ones, from 1848 to 2026. Each one has a date, a plain-language description, a note on why it matters for the Fourth Wave, sources that were opened, a confidence level, and a candidate image from Wikimedia Commons whose licence was checked on the file page.

A machine-readable copy with the same content is in `india-memory.json` (one object per milestone, sorted by date). It can be loaded as an Astro content collection.

---

## 0. Headline findings

1. **The timeline has 40 milestones: 28 India (one of them, the UDHR entry, an India + global bridge) and 12 global.** That is about 70:30. Every milestone has one to four sources that were opened on 2026-10-24, and each source has a date.
2. **There is a strong 2026 ending that is also the hardest to handle.** On 16 April 2026 the government brought the 2023 Women's Reservation Act into force. On 17 April a constitutional amendment that would have let the quota start after a seat redrawing based on the 2011 census failed in the Lok Sabha (298 to 230, short of two-thirds). PRS's analysis says the one-third quota is unlikely to apply at the 2029 general election. This gives Gen Z a clear, trackable demand. It is also live party conflict, so CSP should state facts and one demand (implement the quota) without blaming either side.
3. **The story has a clear spine from 1848 to 2026.** Education (1848) leads to organising (1917, 1927), then the vote (1950), then data (1974: *Towards Equality*, whose authors dissented in favour of reserved seats), then local quotas (1993), then the national quota (2023), then the wait (2026). This is CSP's own pipeline of research, then public consciousness, then political action, told through history.
4. **1848 is a 'two origins' year.** The Phules' girls' school in Pune (January 1848) and the Seneca Falls convention in the USA (19–20 July 1848) happened in the same year. That makes it easy to say feminism started in many places at once, not only in the West.
5. **Verification caught three traps:**
   - The Mathura judgment is dated **15 September 1978** on the court record. Wikipedia says 1979.
   - A search summary said Gowalia Tank Maidan, where Aruna Asaf Ali raised the flag, is 'now Azad Maidan'. No source we opened supports that, so the site should say **Gowalia Tank Maidan**.
   - The widely shared photo of 'Savitribai Phule with Fatima Sheikh' has contested provenance, so it was not used.
6. **Several entries are politically sensitive for a movement that must read as independent.** These are Shah Bano (1985), Sabarimala and Kerala's Women's Wall (2018–19), Shaheen Bagh (2019–20) and the 2023 and 2026 reservation entries. The freedom-movement entries centre Congress-era figures, so keep the focus on what the women did. Each entry has a sensitivity rating, and section 5 gives framing rules.
7. **Survivor-naming and defamation rules apply to five entries** (1978–83, 1997, 2012, 2018, 2024). Indian law restricts identifying survivors of sexual violence, and #MeToo content carries defamation risk. The entries are written so they do not name victims who were not already public figures, or men who were accused but not convicted.
8. **Images:**
   - **35 of the 40 milestones have a usable public-domain, CC0, CC BY or CC BY-SA image**, and 11 have an alternate.
   - Five have none suitable: Tarabai Shinde (1882), Mathura (1978–83), the Domestic Violence Act (2006), the POSH Act (2013) and Pinjra Tod (2015). Use typographic cards for these.
   - Three images need a precise caption because they do not show the event itself: Chipko (a 2023 Pune protest), Iceland (the 2005 strike) and Reclaim the Night (a later march on 4 Sep 2024).
   - All 46 shortlisted files were checked on their Commons file pages. No deletion notices were found.
9. **Today is a ready-made hook.** 24 October 2026 is the 51st anniversary of Iceland's Women's Day Off. 28 September is a double date: the Sabarimala verdict (2018) and assent to the Women's Reservation Act (2023).

## 1. Method and limits

**Sources:**
- Every claim in a description or 'why it matters' line comes from a page I opened with WebFetch or a direct HTTP fetch on 2026-10-24.
- Where possible I used primary or official pages: Supreme Court judgments via Indian Kanoon, UN Treaty Collection, UN, UN Women, FAO, US National Park Service, PRS Legislative Research, President of India, All India Radio, AIWC and SEWA.
- Quality journalism and Wikipedia were used as a second layer. Wikipedia is cited with its revision date.
- Facts seen only in search-engine summaries are labelled **not verified** and kept out of the descriptions.

**Images:** each shortlisted file was checked in two ways.
1. I read its structured licence, author and date through the Wikimedia Commons API (`extmetadata`).
2. I fetched the file page itself and checked that the licence text and author appear on it and that there is no deletion or copyright-violation notice.

Licences accepted: public domain (including CC0 and PD templates), CC BY and CC BY-SA, including ported versions such as CC BY 2.5 DK and CC BY 3.0 CL. GODL-India (the licence on most Indian government stamp and PIB images) was **excluded**, because the brief asked for PD or CC BY/BY-SA only.

**Limits:**
- Britannica, NZHistory, Te Ara, the OHCHR site and several news sites returned HTTP 403 to automated fetches. I used other sources for those facts.
- The shared web-search budget ran out near the end. The POSH Act therefore rests on one source, and some 'not verified' items could not be chased further.
- The PARI copy of *Towards Equality* did not render, so the exact committee dates are unverified.
- I did not view the images by eye, apart from reading their descriptions. Check crops and faces before publishing.
- The earlier UK-focused files (`audience.md`, `peers.md`, `seo-aeo-stack.md`) do not cover this timeline. I did not repeat their content. They are only cross-referenced (the Ni Una Menos domain lapse, the timeline pattern T8).

## 3. Milestones in detail

### 1. Savitribai and Jyotirao Phule open a girls' school in Pune

- **Date:** January 1848 (precision: month)
- **Place / region:** Bhide Wada, Budhwar Peth, Pune · India
- **What happened:** In January 1848 Savitribai Phule, Jyotirao Phule and Sagunabai Kshirsagar started a school for girls at Bhide Wada in Pune, and Savitribai's colleague Fatima Sheikh later opened a school with her in the Sheikh family home.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** Indian feminism starts with a Dalit-Bahujan woman and a Muslim woman claiming education against caste and patriarchy at the same time, so intersectionality here is home-grown, not imported, and education is the first piece of movement infrastructure.
- **Sources:**
  - [Savitribai Phule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savitribai_Phule), Wikipedia, rev. 2026-10-15. Supports: January 1848, Bhidewada, with Sagunabai; Fatima Sheikh school in 1849.
  - [Supreme Court allows national memorial to commemorate first girls' school in India](https://theleaflet.in/supreme-court-allows-national-memorial-to-commemorate-first-girls-school-in-india), The Leaflet, 2023-11-05. Supports: 1848, Bhidewada, Budhwar Peth; SC dismissed occupiers' appeal so the Pune Municipal Corporation can build a memorial.
  - [Activist Dilip Mandal's claim that Fatima Sheikh is a non-existent character is a travesty of historical truth](https://www.altnews.in/activist-dilip-mandals-claim-that-fatima-sheikh-is-a-non-existent-character-is-a-travesty-of-historical-truth/), Alt News, 2025-01-29. Supports: Fatima Sheikh historicity dispute and the evidence for her.
- **Confidence:** high (year and place); medium (exact day; Fatima Sheikh's role)
- **Caveats:** Most sources call this India's first girls' school. Safer wording: 'one of the first girls' schools started by Indians', unless a scholarly history confirms the stronger claim. Fatima Sheikh's existence was publicly questioned in January 2025. Alt News points to a letter Savitribai wrote in 1856 and to books by M.G. Mali (1980, 1988), and it also notes that primary records are thin. Present her with that care. Note for design: the Supreme Court cleared a national memorial at Bhide Wada in 2023.
- **Sensitivity:** medium: caste history; the Fatima Sheikh debate is politically charged
- **Image:** [Jotirao Phule Savitribai Phule Phulewada Pune.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jotirao_Phule_Savitribai_Phule_Phulewada_Pune.jpg)

### 2. Seneca Falls: the first women's rights convention in the US

- **Date:** 19–20 July 1848 (precision: day)
- **Place / region:** Wesleyan Chapel, Seneca Falls, New York · Global (USA)
- **What happened:** At the Wesleyan Chapel in Seneca Falls, New York, on 19–20 July 1848, the first women's rights convention ended with 68 women and 32 men signing the Declaration of Sentiments, drafted by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and the M'Clintock family.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** It happened in the same year the Phules opened their school in Pune. 1848 makes a neat 'two origins' moment showing that feminism started in many places at once, not only in the West.
- **Sources:**
  - [The First Women's Rights Convention](https://www.nps.gov/wori/learn/historyculture/the-first-womens-rights-convention.htm), US National Park Service, updated 2023-02-06. Supports: 19–20 July 1848; 68 women and 32 men signed.
  - [Background: International Women's Day](https://www.un.org/en/observances/womens-day/background), United Nations, undated. Supports: 1848 convention and Declaration of Sentiments.
- **Confidence:** high
- **Sensitivity:** low
- **Image:** [Wesleyan Chapel, Seneca Falls, exterior.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wesleyan_Chapel,_Seneca_Falls,_exterior.jpg)

### 3. Tarabai Shinde publishes 'Stri Purush Tulana'

- **Date:** 1882 (precision: year)
- **Place / region:** Pune (Poona) · India
- **What happened:** Tarabai Shinde's Marathi pamphlet 'Stri Purush Tulana' (A Comparison of Women and Men) turned the tables on writers who blamed women, arguing that men had every fault they pinned on women.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** Calling out double standards has an Indian history of more than 140 years. The pamphlet was met with hostility, written off, and rediscovered only in 1975, which shows why a movement has to archive and amplify women's writing itself.
- **Sources:**
  - [Stri Purush Tulana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stri_Purush_Tulana), Wikipedia, rev. 2026-09-13. Supports: published in Marathi in 1882; republished 1975 by S.G. Malshe; triggered by the Vijayalakshmi case.
  - [Tarabai Shinde: Breaking Caste & Patriarchy Glass Ceilings](https://feminisminindia.com/2017/03/01/remembering-tarabai-shinde-essay/), Feminism in India, 2017-03-01. Supports: content, trigger and hostile reception (article itself gives no year).
- **Confidence:** high
- **Caveats:** Translations of the title vary. Describe it as 'one of the earliest modern feminist texts in India' rather than 'the first'.
- **Sensitivity:** low
- **Image note:** No portrait or cover of the pamphlet was found on Commons (search 'Tarabai Shinde', 2026-10-24). Use a typographic card with the title in Marathi and English.

### 4. Pandita Ramabai opens Sharada Sadan, a home and school for widows

- **Date:** 1889 (precision: year)
- **Place / region:** Bombay or Pune (sources differ) · India
- **What happened:** Pandita Ramabai was the first woman in India to earn the titles 'Pandita' and 'Sarasvati'. In 1889 she opened Sharada Sadan, a place where widowed girls and women could live and study.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** She put shelter, schooling and a route to independence under one roof, funded partly with money she raised abroad. It is an early Indian model of a feminist organisation that both serves people and campaigns.
- **Sources:**
  - [Women's History Month 2020: Pandita Ramabai Sarasvati](https://blogs.soas.ac.uk/archives/?p=5581), SOAS Archives & Special Collections blog, 2020-03-23. Supports: titles Pandita and Sarasvati; Sharada Sadan 1889 in Chowpatty, Bombay; Arya Mahila Samaj in Pune after 1882.
  - [Pandita Ramabai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandita_Ramabai), Wikipedia, rev. 2026-09-01. Supports: 1889 Sharada Sadan for child widows (says Pune); funds raised abroad; Mukti Mission.
- **Confidence:** high (year); low (location)
- **Caveats:** The sources disagree on the location: SOAS says Chowpatty, Bombay, and Wikipedia says Pune. Confirm before printing a place. Her later Christian mission work was controversial in her time, so present it factually.
- **Sensitivity:** low to medium: religious conversion
- **Image:** [Pandita Ramabai herself (a photo from the book).jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pandita_Ramabai_herself_(a_photo_from_the_book).jpg)

### 5. New Zealand becomes the first self-governing country where women can vote

- **Date:** 19 September 1893 (precision: day)
- **Place / region:** Wellington · Global (New Zealand)
- **What happened:** On 19 September 1893 New Zealand's governor signed the Electoral Act, making it the first self-governing country where women could vote in parliamentary elections, after a petition of 31,872 signatures organised by Kate Sheppard and the WCTU.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** It shows petitions plus persistence work. Māori and Pākehā women both voted in the elections of 1893.
- **Sources:**
  - [Suffrage petition submitted to Parliament on 28 July 1893](https://digitalnz.org/stories/6690b848465c01001ecee026), DigitalNZ (National Library of New Zealand), undated. Supports: 31,872 signatures; Act signed 19 Sep 1893; 'first self-governing country'.
  - [Women's suffrage in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women%27s_suffrage_in_New_Zealand), Wikipedia, rev. 2026-09-09. Supports: assent 19 Sep 1893; women voted 28 Nov 1893; Māori electorates 20 Dec 1893.
- **Confidence:** high
- **Caveats:** Keep the qualifier 'self-governing country': New Zealand was then a British colony.
- **Sensitivity:** low

### 6. Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain imagines 'Ladyland'

- **Date:** 1905 (precision: year)
- **Place / region:** Published in Madras; author from Bengal · India (Bengal)
- **What happened:** Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain's 'Sultana's Dream' was first published in The Indian Ladies' Magazine in Madras in 1905. It imagines Ladyland, a science-fiction utopia where women run everything and men are kept in seclusion.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** Imagining the world you want is itself a political act. The story is among the earliest known Indian science fiction in English, and a model for CSP's creative and speculative work.
- **Sources:**
  - [Sultana's Dream by Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (review)](https://strangehorizons.com/wordpress/non-fiction/reviews/sultanas-dream-by-rokeya-sakhawat-hossain/), Strange Horizons, 2013-09-30. Supports: Indian Ladies Magazine, Madras, 1905; book 1908; among earliest Indian SF in English.
  - [Sultana's Dream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultana%27s_Dream), Wikipedia, rev. 2026-09-12. Supports: Ladyland; men secluded, a mirror image of purdah.
- **Confidence:** high
- **Caveats:** Bangladesh also claims Rokeya as part of its heritage, so the region tag could read 'Bengal'.
- **Sensitivity:** low

### 8. Petrograd women strike for 'bread and peace'

- **Date:** 8 March 1917 (23 February in the old Russian calendar) (precision: day)
- **Place / region:** Petrograd (St Petersburg) · Global (Russia)
- **What happened:** On 8 March 1917 (23 February in Russia's old calendar), women in Petrograd went on strike for 'bread and peace'. Within days the Tsar abdicated, and the new provisional government granted women the vote.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** This strike is why International Women's Day falls on 8 March: women's economic protest helped topple an empire.
- **Sources:**

- **Caveats:** The UN wording runs the abdication and the vote together. 'Later granted' is the safer phrasing.

### 9. Women's Indian Association founded; women ask for the vote

- **Date:** 1917 (deputation 15 December 1917) (precision: year)
- **Place / region:** Adyar, Madras · India
- **What happened:** The Women's Indian Association was founded in Adyar, Madras, in 1917. That December an all-women delegation asked the Montagu–Chelmsford inquiry to give Indian women the vote.
- **Why it matters for the Fourth Wave:** This was the first organised campaign for Indian women's suffrage. The vote was then won step by step: Madras city in 1919, and the Madras and Bombay presidencies in 1921. Rights came from persistent organising, not a single event.

## 4. Image crediting rules and credit list

**How to credit (for the Astro build):**
1. **CC BY and CC BY-SA files need a visible credit**, next to the image or in a captions or credits list linked from it. The credit should give the author, the licence name linked to the licence, and a link to the Commons file page.
   - Pattern: `Author, CC BY-SA 4.0 (linked), via Wikimedia Commons (linked to file page)`.
   - The Meghalaya photo requires its own exact string: `Evan Centanni / www.polgeonow.com / CC BY 4.0`.
2. **ShareAlike (BY-SA):** if CSP crops, recolours, adds text to or collages a BY-SA image, the edited image has to be released under the same BY-SA licence. Say so in the credit, for example 'cropped and tinted by CSP, CC BY-SA 4.0'. CSP's own text and layout stay unaffected.
3. **Public domain and CC0:** no credit is legally required, but crediting is good practice and helps public memory. Several 'PD' files are PD-US only, or rely on Indian term rules: Margaret Cousins (PD-US), Pandita Ramabai (PD-US), and the 1914 poster (artist's death date uncertain). The site is aimed at India, so treat these as medium certainty.
4. **People in photos:** the Shaheen Bagh, Women's Wall, Reclaim the Night and Ni Una Menos photos show identifiable members of the public at political protests.
   - Prefer crowd shots or crops where faces are not prominent.
   - Never use these images in fundraising or advertising contexts that imply those people endorse CSP.
5. **Self-host copies.** Download each file, convert it to AVIF or WebP at the size you need, and keep the credit with it in the JSON. Do not hot-link to `upload.wikimedia.org`.

## 5. Editorial guidance for CSP

**Staying independent:**
- Lead each entry with what women did, not with which party governed.
- Where a party was involved, name it once, factually and neutrally. Examples: the 1986 Act after Shah Bano was passed by the Congress government of the day; Kerala's Women's Wall was organised by the state government with the ruling front.
- Even-handed treatment of Congress-era decisions is itself evidence of independence, which matters after the 2024 'ex-Congress' X thread (see `founder.md`).
- Present the 2026 reservation entry as 'law in force, quota not yet operational'. Pair it with one demand and no blame.

**Sensitivity tiers** (as rated in the entries):
- **Very high:** Sabarimala and the Women's Wall, Shaheen Bagh, the 2026 reservation entry. Recheck the status the week you publish, keep the wording factual, and consider a short 'why we include this' note.
- **High:** Shah Bano, Mathura, Vishaka, the December 2012 protests, #MeTooIndia, Reclaim the Night, the 2023 Act, Pelicot. Follow the survivor-naming and defamation rules below.
- **Low or medium:** everything else.

**Survivor naming and defamation:**
- Do not name victims of sexual violence unless they chose to be public figures. Bhanwari Devi, Priya Ramani and Gisèle Pelicot did. In the 2012 Delhi case the mother has named her daughter, but take legal advice.
- Never name the RG Kar victim.
- Do not name men who were accused but not convicted in #MeToo content.

**Tone for Gen Z:**
- One sentence on what happened, one on why it matters.
- Use active verbs and avoid jargon.
- Keep the hedges the sources use ('widely credited', 'reported').
- Write positively, not angrily: celebrate the wins and name the unfinished business plainly.
