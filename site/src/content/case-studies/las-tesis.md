*Content note: this page discusses rape, sexual violence by state agents and police violence.*

## Context

LASTESIS is a feminist performance collective formed in 2018 in Valparaíso, a port city on Chile's Pacific coast. Its name refers to theses: the idea is to take the arguments of feminist thinkers out of books and put them on the street, where anyone can see them.

Its first piece drew on the Italian-American scholar Silvia Federici. The next drew on the Argentine anthropologist Rita Segato, whose research treats rape not as a private crime by a few men but as an act that a whole social and political order makes possible.

In October 2019, Chile erupted in a mass social uprising. As the protests grew, Chile's National Human Rights Institute began documenting sexual violence by state agents in the context of the protests: 194 cases by December, according to Al Jazeera. That was the moment the collective chose to perform.

## The story

On 20 November 2019, the collective staged the piece for the first time in front of a police station in Valparaíso. It was short, simple and built to be learned quickly: a line of women, blindfolded, moving in unison and chanting. The words take blame away from the woman (what she wore, where she was, what time it was) and place it on the system that protects her attacker: police, courts, the state.

Five days later, on 25 November, the International Day for the Elimination of Violence against Women, it was performed in Santiago, including outside the Supreme Court. Videos spread fast on social media, and then something unusual happened. Women everywhere started learning it.

### More than 200 cities in a month

From Lima to Oaxaca, groups translated the words, adapted them and performed them in their own streets. In Istanbul, police detained performers; women members of Turkey's parliament then performed it inside the parliament building. By 20 December 2019 the piece had been staged in more than 200 cities in over 40 countries, on every continent except Antarctica, according to a crowd-sourced map backed by GeoChicas, a collective of women mappers. By 2021 the map listed more than 400 locations.

### Recognition, and a reaction

In 2020 LASTESIS was named in the TIME100, in a tribute written by Nadya Tolokonnikova of Pussy Riot, who noted that the piece had been performed in more than 52 countries. The collective also won Chile's Jaime Castillo Velasco human rights award and a Design of the Year award from London's Design Museum.

Recognition brought pressure too. After the collective made a video with Pussy Riot in 2020, police filed a complaint against its members. Front Line Defenders, which supports human rights defenders at risk, tracked the case until it was dismissed in January 2021.

### What changed, and what didn't

The protests had opened a process to write a new constitution. On 4 March 2020, both chambers of Congress approved gender parity for electing the Constitutional Convention: the first rule of its kind in the world for a body writing a constitution. Commentators link the performance's momentum to that reform, though no single action can claim it.

The convention met, wrote a draft and put it to the people. On 4 September 2022, voters rejected it by 62% to 38%.

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

LASTESIS solved a problem every movement has: how do you move an idea out of a research paper and into a million bodies? They did not simplify the theory. They gave it a shape, a rhythm and a rule anyone could follow.

India already knows this power. Street theatre, nukkad natak, has carried political ideas through bastis, campuses and bus stands for generations. A Fourth Wave movement can build on that tradition: take one finding from the Receipts Desk, turn it into a five-minute piece anyone can perform, publish the kit openly, and map every place it reaches.

The hard lesson is that a viral moment is not a law. Chile's parity rule was a real gain, and the draft constitution still lost. Copy the joy and the format; plan just as carefully for what happens after the cameras leave.
