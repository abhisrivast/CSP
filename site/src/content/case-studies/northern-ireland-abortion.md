*Content note: this case study discusses abortion, including after rape, and pregnancies with fatal foetal abnormality.*

## Context

In England, Scotland and Wales, abortion has been legal under set conditions since the Abortion Act 1967. That Act never applied in Northern Ireland.

There, sections 58 and 59 of the Offences Against the Person Act 1861, a law written when Queen Victoria was on the throne, still made almost every abortion a crime. Section 58 carried a possible life sentence.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

The result was easy to count. In the whole of 2017–18, Northern Ireland's hospitals recorded 12 terminations.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> Most people who needed one had to travel to Britain for care, if they could afford it.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Then politics stalled completely. From 2017 to 2020, Northern Ireland had no devolved government at all: the power-sharing Executive at Stormont was suspended for three years.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> For campaigners that looked like a dead end. It turned out to be an opening.

## The story

### Court yes, Assembly no

Campaigners tried two doors at once. In November 2015, a High Court judge in Belfast declared the law incompatible with human-rights law where a pregnancy had a fatal foetal abnormality or came from rape or incest.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Three months later, in February 2016, the Northern Ireland Assembly voted down a proposal to allow abortion in cases of fatal foetal abnormality, 59 votes to 40. Another bill on the same issue fell when the Assembly was suspended in January 2017, and that June the Court of Appeal overturned the Belfast declaration.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

### All the way up

The case went to the top. On 7 June 2018, a majority of the UK Supreme Court agreed with the campaigners on the big question: the law was incompatible with the right to respect for private and family life in cases of rape, incest and fatal foetal abnormality. But a different majority ruled that the Northern Ireland Human Rights Commission had no standing to bring the case, so no formal declaration could be made.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Right argument. Wrong claimant. Still a powerful piece of evidence.

### Into the UN, and across the border

Campaigners had also gone to Geneva. In February 2018, the UN Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women, the body that oversees CEDAW, published the report of its inquiry into the UK. It recommended liberalising Northern Ireland's abortion law.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Then Ireland voted. On 25 May 2018, voters in the Republic of Ireland repealed their constitutional ban on abortion, with 66.4% voting yes.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup> Women in Repeal jumpers celebrated at Dublin Castle. The UK government's answer for Northern Ireland was that abortion there was a devolved matter, and it would not step in.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

Now there were two authoritative findings on the table, one from the UK's highest court and one from a UN treaty body, and a neighbour that had just voted for change.

### The lever in another parliament

With Stormont empty, power over Northern Ireland sat in Westminster. In July 2019, MPs were passing a bill to extend the deadline for restoring the Executive. Stella Creasy MP added an amendment: if no Executive was back by 21 October 2019, the UK government had to implement the CEDAW recommendations, and sections 58 and 59 of the 1861 Act would be repealed for Northern Ireland.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup><sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> The Act became law on 24 July 2019.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

In October, a Belfast High Court judge ruled the old law breached human-rights law, but made no formal declaration, because the Act was about to take effect.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> At midnight on 21 October, the deadline passed with no Executive. Abortion was decriminalised.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### From law to clinic

New regulations took effect on 31 March 2020. Abortion is now permitted for any reason in the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, with limits after that.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> But a right on paper is not a clinic. In May 2022, two years on, services were still limited and women were still travelling to Great Britain. By 2024, services were available in all five health trusts and most demand could be met locally.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Today, people in Northern Ireland are referred through the British Pregnancy Advisory Service to one of those trusts, and eight safe access zones protect patients and staff outside clinics.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup> Alliance for Choice runs an information line and abortion doulas, and keeps campaigning.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Northern Ireland proves that power does not always sit where you expect. When the local government vanished, campaigners did not wait for it to come back. They found the one lever left, an amendment in another parliament, and justified it with evidence from the courts and the UN.

For a movement based in India, this is directly useful. The European Convention on Human Rights is not Indian law, but CEDAW binds India too: India ratified it in 1993. Shadow reports to the CEDAW Committee are open to Indian groups, and they create a public record that governments have to answer.

And it is a warning. A law won from outside must be followed by services inside. Keep counting clinics, waiting times and safe access zones long after the celebration.
