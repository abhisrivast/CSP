*Content note: this page discusses sexual harassment and sexual violence.*

## Context

Long before it was a hashtag, “me too” was a promise between survivors. In 2006 Tarana Burke, a Black community organiser in the United States, started using the phrase in her work with young women of colour who had survived sexual violence. The idea was simple: you are not alone, and there is a path to healing.

For eleven years the phrase lived mostly in that work. Then, in early October 2017, investigative reports exposed allegations of sexual abuse against Harvey Weinstein, a powerful Hollywood producer. The reporting described a pattern that many women recognised from their own offices, campuses and families: power on one side, fear and silence on the other. The question many people asked next was not whether this happened in film, but how common it was everywhere else.

## The story

On 15 October 2017, the actor Alyssa Milano posted an invitation on Twitter: if you have been sexually harassed or assaulted, reply “me too”. She later reported 32,000 replies within 24 hours. On Facebook, 4.7 million people made more than 12 million posts, comments and reactions in less than a day. The scale was the message.

Two words worked because they asked so little and said so much. You did not need to name anyone, tell the whole story or prove anything. You only had to stand up and be counted. Millions did, in dozens of languages. By December TIME had named “The Silence Breakers” its Person of the Year and counted the hashtag in at least 85 countries.

### From posts to data

Testimony at that scale became evidence. Pew Research Center found the hashtag had been used more than 19 million times on Twitter in under a year: about 55,319 times a day. Its analysis also showed the gaps. In peak periods, 71% of #MeToo tweets were in English, and 15% were about celebrities or entertainment. Even inside a global moment, some voices carried further than others.

### India’s #MeToo

In September 2018, an interview given by the actor Tanushree Dutta set off a wave of public accounts of harassment in India, across film, media and politics. One case became a landmark. The former Union minister M.J. Akbar filed a criminal defamation case against the journalist Priya Ramani over her account. On 17 February 2021 a Delhi court acquitted her, holding that a woman has the right to raise her grievance even after decades. Newspapers across India reported it as a landmark for women who speak up.

### The long road through the courts

In the United States, Harvey Weinstein was convicted in New York in 2020. In April 2024 the state’s highest court overturned those convictions and ordered a new trial. At the 2025 retrial he was convicted on one count and acquitted on another, and the jury could not agree on a third. A second retrial on that count also ended without a verdict, and on 25 June 2026 prosecutors said they would not try it again.

Tarana Burke, meanwhile, publicly challenged the way Black women’s foundational work was being written out of the story, even as she welcomed the wider movement. The movement began with survivors, long before it had celebrities.

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

#MeToo proved that testimony, gathered at scale, can change what a society treats as normal in weeks. It also showed how quickly attention moves on, and how slowly courts, workplaces and laws catch up.

For a Fourth Wave movement in India, the lesson is to build the infrastructure the hashtag did not have. India’s workplaces already have a legal framework against sexual harassment, the POSH Act of 2013, which grew out of the Supreme Court’s Vishaka guidelines of 1997 (read our case on [Bhanwari Devi, Vishaka and the POSH Act](/case-studies/bhanwari-devi-vishaka-posh/)). A movement can help people use it: plain-language guides, private and safe ways to share experiences, legal advice before anything is published, and research that turns many private stories into one public demand.

And it should always credit where the work began. Every hashtag has an organiser behind it.
