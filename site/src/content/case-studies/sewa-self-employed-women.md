## Context

Most women in India who earn a living have no boss, no payslip and no union. They sell vegetables, carry loads on their heads, roll bidis, sew at home, sort waste and work the fields. SEWA's own website puts the share of India's labour force in the informal economy at 93%.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Ela Bhatt, born in Ahmedabad in 1933, joined the legal department of the Textile Labour Association (TLA) in 1955.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> The TLA, founded in 1920 by Anasuya Sarabhai and Mahatma Gandhi, organised the city's mill workers. SEWA would grow out of its Women's Wing.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

When two mills closed, Bhatt went to see how the laid-off workers' families were managing. She found the women holding them up: buying mill waste, sorting the rags and stitching them into quilts and covers to sell. It was long, hard, badly paid work, and no union counted it.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

That is where the idea started. If these women were workers, they deserved a union too.

## The story

### A union with no boss

SEWA was registered as a trade union under the Trade Unions Act, 1926, on 12 April 1972, registration number G.938.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> It began with 80 women in Ahmedabad: head-loaders, vendors, home-based workers, producers and service providers.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> The idea was radical for its time: a union for workers with no single employer to bargain with. SEWA still marks 12 April as Self-Employed Day.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

### "We are so many"

The first enemy was debt. A vegetable vendor would borrow ₹50 from a moneylender in the morning, buy stock, sell it at the Manek Chowk market and pay back ₹55 that evening: interest of 10% a day. The newly nationalised banks did not want poor women walking in.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

At a meeting on the riverbank at Naranghat, Chandaben, who mended old clothes in the Raikhad market, said they should make their own bank. Bhatt asked where poor women would find the capital. As Bhatt told it fifty years later, Chandaben replied:<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

> "So what if we are poor, but we are so many."

The women each put in ₹10 of share capital. Then the registrar objected that most of the founding board could not sign their names. So Bhatt wrote each woman's name and trade on a slate, hung the slates around their necks, photographed them and went back. This time the registrar could not say no. SEWA Bank was registered in 1974.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### A banyan tree

SEWA describes what grew next as a banyan tree. The union fights for members' rights as workers; cooperatives let them own and manage their trades.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> Today members run cooperatives of health workers, construction workers and waste pickers, and in 2021–22 SEWA Bank held 3,51,453 accounts and about ₹330 crore in deposits. It is democratic by design: members elect one representative for every 1,500 of them, and those representatives elect a 25-member executive.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### From the market to the law

The fights went to the courts. Indira Jaising, who argued SEWA's cases, recalls a Supreme Court ruling that Manek Chowk's vegetable vendors could not be evicted unless they were moved to a new municipal market on the same site, and a Delhi High Court win that put SEWA at labour negotiating tables it had been kept away from because its members were not "employed".<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

They went to Parliament and beyond. Bhatt served in the Rajya Sabha from 1986 to 1989 and chaired a national commission on self-employed women.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> She lobbied for the International Labour Organization's Home Work Convention, adopted in Geneva on 20 June 1996, which set standards for people paid to work from home.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a>,<a href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup> And in March 2014, India's Street Vendors Act gave vendors legal protection for their livelihoods.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

### Fifty years on

On 12 April 2022, Ela Bhatt celebrated SEWA's 50 years with leaders from 18 states. She died on 2 November that year. SEWA's membership in 2022 was 25,32,846, and 35% of members were from the young generation.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> Its website now lists 3.78 million members in 20 states.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

SEWA is proof that feminism can be infrastructure: a union card, a bank account, a cooperative, a seat at the table. It did not wait for anyone to recognise women’s work. It organised the women and made recognition unavoidable.

For the Fourth Wave, that is a template. Much of our generation’s work is informal too: gig shifts, freelancing, family businesses, creator work and unpaid care. The questions SEWA asked in 1972 still apply. Who is the employer? Who pays when you fall sick? Where do you save? Who speaks for you at the table?

And SEWA judges itself by what members gain, not by headlines: more work, more income, food, health, childcare, housing, assets, leadership and self-reliance. That is a scoreboard worth borrowing. Hope first, receipts always.
