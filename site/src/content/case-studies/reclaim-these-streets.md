*Content note: this case study discusses the kidnap, rape and murder of a woman by a police officer, and police force at a vigil.*

## Context

On the evening of 3 March 2021, Sarah Everard, 33, was walking home through south London from a friend's place near Clapham Common. A man stopped her, said he was a police officer and handcuffed her. He was. Wayne Couzens, a serving Metropolitan Police constable, drove her to Kent, where he raped and murdered her.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Britain was in a Covid lockdown. Across the country, women started posting the same sentence they had been saying to each other for years: *text me when you get home*.

Four women in London, Jessica Leigh, Anna Birley, Henna Shah and Jamie Klingler, decided grief needed a place to go. They planned a vigil on Clapham Common, close to where she had been kidnapped, for 13 March, and called themselves Reclaim These Streets.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> Their stated aim was bigger than one evening: to use law, education and community action so that no woman would have to be asked to text a friend she got home safely again.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

## The story

### The vigil that wasn’t

Reclaim These Streets did what organisers are supposed to do. They told the police. The Met's answer was that under Covid rules, the organisers could be fined.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> An urgent High Court hearing on 12 March did not give them the protection they needed, and they called the vigil off.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

### Clapham Common, 13 March

People came anyway. All day, hundreds left flowers at the bandstand on Clapham Common, among them the then Duchess of Cambridge.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

In the evening, a crowd of several hundred gathered at the bandstand for speeches organised by the direct-action group Sisters Uncut. Police moved in to break it up, and four people were arrested.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> A vigil for a woman killed by a police officer had been broken up by police, and the police response was widely criticised.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> Vigils went ahead in other cities, and the next day more than 1,000 people marched from New Scotland Yard to Parliament Square.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

### Taking the Met to court

The four founders did not let it go. They asked the High Court to review the Met’s decisions before the vigil.

On 11 March 2022, almost exactly a year later, the court ruled that six of those decisions were unlawful. The police had failed to consider whether going to a vigil could be a reasonable excuse under Covid rules, given people's rights to freedom of expression and assembly under Articles 10 and 11 of the European Convention on Human Rights.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> The Met tried to appeal and was refused, first by the High Court and then by the Court of Appeal.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> Women had been silenced, the group said, and now a court had confirmed that the police were wrong to do it.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### The bigger reckoning

The murder, and that night, opened a much wider argument about policing and women.

Couzens pleaded guilty, and on 30 September 2021 he was given a whole-life order.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> In March 2023, a review led by Baroness Casey found the Met institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup> On 29 February 2024, Part 1 of the Angiolini Inquiry, into how Couzens kept his job despite earlier allegations, reported red flags and missed opportunities across his career and made 16 recommendations on vetting, recruitment, indecent exposure and police culture.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

At the same time, the state was moving the other way on protest. The Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Bill, which gave police wider powers to set conditions on demonstrations, sparked "Kill the Bill" protests across Britain. It became law on 28 April 2022.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Reclaim These Streets shows that a courtroom can be a street too. When the state closes the space to gather, litigation can reopen it, and the precedent protects everyone who protests next.

It also shows how grief becomes a demand. A sentence women whisper to each other, *text me when you get home*, became a public argument about who keeps women safe, and who keeps the keepers honest.

And it carries a warning for any movement, in London or in Delhi. When a police officer commits the crime, the official answer can still be more police powers and more surveillance. Feminists need their own safety agenda ready: lighting, transport, prevention and real accountability. And they need to own their infrastructure, from the domain name to the archive, so the record outlives the moment.
