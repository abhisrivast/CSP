*Content note: this case study discusses the killing of women and girls, including by partners, and police force against peaceful protesters.*

## Context

Around the world, the most dangerous place for a woman is often her own home. In 2024, about 83,000 women and girls were killed intentionally. Some 50,000 of them, 60%, were killed by a partner or a family member: 137 every day. Africa had the highest rate of partner and family femicide of any region.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

Femicide means the killing of a woman or girl because she is a woman or girl. Kenya had no proper national system for counting it, and the law does not treat it as a separate crime.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup><sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-8" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup> Without a count, each killing could be reported as a one-off: a domestic dispute, a tragedy, a crime of passion. Nobody had to add them up.

In January 2024, after a run of killings of young women, Kenyans decided to add them up themselves.

## The story

### Thousands in the streets

At the end of January 2024, thousands of people marched in cities and towns across Kenya, called out by a coalition of organisations. Africanews described it as the largest anti-femicide demonstration ever held in the country.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> At its centre was a coalition called EndFemicideKE, whose activism, the scholar Awino Okech later wrote, reshaped the national debate.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### Dark Valentine

On 14 February, hundreds of people dressed in black gathered in Nairobi for a candlelit vigil they called Dark Valentine. They held red roses and chanted the names of women who had been killed. Police and local media had reported more than 16 femicides since New Year.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> One student activist put it simply: love should never be something that puts you at risk of death.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

### Counting what the state would not

This is where #EndFemicideKE became more than a protest.

With no official count, data teams, journalists and feminist groups built their own. Africa Data Hub, working with Africa Uncensored and Odipodev, combed through court judgments and news reports to build *Silencing Women*: at least 1,069 killings of women, 842 of which meet the UNODC definition of femicide. In 80% of cases, the killer was a current or former partner.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> Femicide Count Kenya and the Heinrich Böll Foundation's Nairobi office kept their own tallies.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup><sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

Suddenly the killings were not one-offs. They were a pattern, with a method anyone could check.

### The state moves, partly

The killings did not stop. In September 2024, the Olympic marathon runner Rebecca Cheptegei died in hospital in Eldoret, four days after her partner poured petrol on her and set her alight ([Africanews, 5 Sep 2024](https://www.africanews.com/2024/09/05/ugandan-olympic-athlete-dies-after-being-severely-burned-by-her-partner-over-a-land-disput/)). Police recorded 97 femicide cases between September and November.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

In December, during the 16 Days of Activism against gender-based violence, police tear-gassed a peaceful march in Nairobi.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> On 19 December, the government called gender-based violence the country's biggest security issue. It cited about 100 women killed in four months and 7,107 reports of sexual and gender-based violence since September 2023. Police set up a femicide unit, and the Cabinet approved a presidential group to propose solutions.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

In January 2025 came a 42-member technical working group. Okech warned that it was too big, too costly and too broad, and that it left out the campaigners who had changed the national debate.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### Still counting

Civil society counted at least 220 femicides in 2025, 129 of them in the first three months. After March, the government stopped publishing consolidated figures.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup> In August 2025, the anti-violence organisation Usikimye opened MASKAN, an exhibition in Nairobi that turned the dead into public memory ([Wikimedia Commons record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:MASKAN_exhibition_01.jpg)).

On 21 May 2026, campaigners gave the government 40 days to act. Two days later, police formed a second specialist investigation unit. On 1 June, hundreds marched through Nairobi with placards that read "Stop Killing Women".<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

#EndFemicideKE shows, in real time, that counting is power. When a government will not count, a movement can: with a clear definition, a public method and journalists as partners. That data made the killings impossible to wave away as private tragedies, and it pushed the state to name the crisis out loud.

It also shows that naming is not fixing. Femicide is still not a separate crime. Official data went dark in 2025. A peaceful march met tear gas.

So the Fourth Wave needs both halves: the memorial and the tracker. Name the women. Count them, carefully. Commemorate them in public. And publish every promise the state makes, with its date and its status, until it is kept.
