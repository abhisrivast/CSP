*Content note: this page discusses femicide, the killing of women and girls because of their gender.*

## Context

Until 2015, Argentina had no official count of femicides. The best national figure came from an NGO, La Casa del Encuentro, which tallied killings reported in the media. If the state did not count the dead, it could hardly be asked to prevent the next death.

The words that changed this came from further north. In 1995 the Mexican poet Susana Chávez wrote “Ni una mujer menos, ni una muerte más” (not one woman less, not one more death) to protest the killings of women in Ciudad Juárez. Chávez was herself murdered in 2011.

In March 2015, a group of journalists, writers, artists and academics in Buenos Aires organised a reading marathon against femicide. The activist Vanina Escales proposed a name for it, shortened from Chávez's line: Ni una menos. It was a cultural event, not a protest. Within weeks it would become both.

## The story

In May 2015, a 14-year-old girl was murdered. Her death became the spark. The hashtag #NiUnaMenos spread, and on 3 June 2015 people marched in about 80 cities across Argentina. In Buenos Aires the march centred on the square in front of Congress; one estimate put the crowd at about 200,000. A list of demands to the state was read out from the stage.

The state responded. A Supreme Court justice, Elena Highton de Nolasco, announced that the court would build a national register of femicides. It began that year, and has published a report for every year since. For the first time Argentina had official numbers: for 2017, 251 direct victims, about 60% killed by a partner or ex-partner, 71% in their own homes.

### From marches to strikes

The movement kept inventing new forms. On 19 October 2016, after the murder of a 16-year-old girl, women across Argentina stopped work for one hour, from 13:00 to 14:00, on what they called Black Wednesday. About 50 organisations took part. On 8 March 2017 Ni Una Menos helped convene the first International Women's Strike, and the slogan travelled to Uruguay, Mexico, Peru, Bolivia, Colombia, Venezuela, and on to Spain and Italy.

### The green wave

By 2018 the movement had widened its demands. Its banner on 4 June read “Without legal abortion there is no Ni Una Menos”, and about 150,000 people marched behind it. The green scarf of the national campaign for legal abortion became a second symbol. That August the bill failed in the Senate.

Activists did not let go. Researchers who studied the campaign argue that Ni Una Menos had built the organisational base and the social-justice framing that made abortion a mainstream demand: poorer women were the ones dying from unsafe, illegal abortions. On 30 December 2020, Congress passed Law 27.610, legalising abortion on request up to and including the 14th week.

### Training the state

In between, the movement won a quieter law. Micaela García, a young Ni Una Menos activist, was murdered in 2017. The law named after her, Ley Micaela, passed the Chamber of Deputies 171 to 1 in December 2018 and was promulgated in January 2019. Everyone working in the executive, legislature and judiciary now has to be trained on gender and gender-based violence.

### When the state walks back

In December 2023 a new government dissolved the Ministry of Women, Genders and Diversity by decree, and closed its successor office in June 2024. Ni Una Menos marched against the cuts that 3 June. A proposal to shrink Ley Micaela was dropped from the final version of the government's flagship reform law in 2024. The register keeps counting. And on 3 June 2026, people marched again, eleven years after the first time.

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Ni Una Menos shows what a movement can do with a number. By demanding that the state count, it turned grief into evidence, and evidence into laws that outlasted the government that passed them.

India has its own counting problem. Reports on the National Crime Records Bureau's *Crime in India 2024* put dowry deaths alone at 5,737: about 16 women a day. Behind each figure is a family, a police file and a court case that may take years. A Fourth Wave movement in India can do what Ni Una Menos did: publish its own careful count, with its method in the open, and campaign for better official data at the same time.

One more lesson, about memory. The movement's old website address now serves an online casino page (checked 24 October 2026). The marches live on; the archive did not. Own your domain, your data and your story.
