*Content note: this case study discusses a death in custody, the killing of protesters, including children, and executions. To protect people at risk, we show no identifiable protesters from inside Iran: only murals, typography and solidarity rallies abroad.*

## Context

Since soon after the 1979 revolution, Iranian law has required women to cover their hair in public. A "Guidance Patrol", the morality police, enforces it on the street.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

On 13 September 2022, that patrol arrested Jina Mahsa Amini, a 22-year-old Kurdish Iranian woman visiting Tehran from her hometown of Saqqez, for allegedly wearing her hijab "improperly". Eyewitnesses said officers beat her; the authorities denied it. She collapsed, and died in hospital three days later.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> In March 2024, a UN fact-finding mission said the Iranian state bore responsibility for her death.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The words that rose after her death were older than she was. *Jin, Jiyan, Azadî*, woman, life, freedom, comes from the Kurdish women's movement of the late 20th century.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> In Persian it became *Zan, Zendegi, Azadi*. Within weeks it was being chanted, painted and printed in many languages.

## The story

### At the funeral

At her funeral in Saqqez, women pulled off their headscarves, threw them into the air and chanted the slogan. Someone posted it; it trended. Then it was heard in the streets of Sanandaj, and then everywhere.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

### A country on its feet

The protests spread from Kurdistan across Iran. They reached every social class, and universities, streets and schools; schoolgirls demonstrated in numbers for the first time.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> Women cut their hair in public and burned their headscarves. People chanted from windows and rooftops.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The government answered with internet blackouts, restrictions on social media, tear gas and gunfire.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

A song carried what the streets could not. Shervin Hajipour released "Baraye" on 28 September; its closing words carry the slogan, and millions watched it.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> On 1 October it was sung at solidarity protests in about 150 cities around the world.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> Three days later, he was arrested.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### The cost

By the first anniversary, rights groups based outside Iran counted more than 500 people killed, including at least 70 minors. Seven people had been executed after protest-related convictions.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> Before a mass pardon in February 2023, an estimated 19,262 people had been arrested across at least 134 cities and towns and 132 universities.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The morality police returned. Smart cameras were set to spot uncovered hair in streets and cars. Hijab stayed mandatory.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> In September 2024, Iran's Guardian Council approved a harsher "Hijab and Chastity" law, with more fines, longer prison terms and limits on jobs and education.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup> Its implementation was put on hold in December 2024, when the Supreme National Security Council advised against enacting it. In May 2025, parliament's speaker said the council had told parliament in writing not to enact it "for the time being" ([Rudaw, 25 May 2025](https://www.rudaw.net/english/middleeast/iran/25052025)).

### Taking the record to the world

But the movement did something repression could not undo: it made the world look, and keep looking.

In November 2022, the UN Human Rights Council set up an independent fact-finding mission.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup> In October 2023, the imprisoned activist Narges Mohammadi won the Nobel Peace Prize<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-8" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup>, and the European Parliament gave its Sakharov Prize to Jina Mahsa Amini and the Woman, Life, Freedom movement.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> The mission’s report, in March 2024, laid the responsibility for her death and for the crackdown on the state.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Then, on 16 September 2026, Amnesty International published a report of about 1,000 pages. It found crimes against humanity, identified 377 people killed in 2022, including 56 children, and named 87 officials it believes should face international justice. It said the crackdown on protests earlier in 2026 followed the same pattern, on an even deadlier scale.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Woman, Life, Freedom is one of the clearest proofs this decade that a feminist slogan can carry a whole country’s demand for freedom. Not a list of grievances: three affirmative words, with women’s lives at the centre.

It also teaches the ethics of solidarity. When people inside a country risk prison or worse for being seen, the job of everyone outside is to amplify without exposing. Credit the Kurdish women’s movement the words came from. Share murals, typography and verified reports, not faces. Push your own institutions to act on UN findings.

And it shows why documentation is a feminist act. Counts, dates, names of officials, methods published in the open: that is how a movement makes sure that what was done can never be quietly forgotten.
