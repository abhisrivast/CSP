*Content note: this case study discusses the rape and murder of a doctor at her workplace. We do not name her or show images of her: Indian law protects a survivor's identity, and the Supreme Court has said it must not be published.*

## Context

On 9 August 2024, a postgraduate trainee doctor was found dead at RG Kar Medical College and Hospital in Kolkata. She had been raped and murdered while on duty, inside the hospital where she worked.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The news hit a nerve every working woman knows. Nurses, doctors, call-centre staff, students and shift workers hear the same advice all their lives: don't stay late, don't go out at night, don't be alone. Here was a woman at work, in a government hospital, and still she was not safe.

Junior doctors stopped work.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup> Grief and anger spread across Bengal, then the country. And one woman decided that this time the answer to fear of the night would not be to stay at home. It would be to go out into it, together.

## The story

### One call, one time

Rimjhim Sinha's call spread on social media and through WhatsApp groups: *Meyera, Raat Dakhal Karo*, "Girls, reclaim the night".<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>,<a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> The instruction was simple enough for anyone to follow. Be on the street at 11:55 pm on 14 August, five minutes before Independence Day.

### The night

Thousands came. In Kolkata they gathered at Jadavpur's 8B bus stand, College Square, Shyambazar, the Academy of Fine Arts and Sealdah station; about 8,000 people stood under the Biswa Bangla Gate in New Town. Women marched in Siliguri, Bardhaman, Krishnanagar, Santiniketan, Cooch Behar, Diamond Harbour and more.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup> In Delhi there were solidarity marches outside AIIMS and in Chittaranjan Park.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

It sounded like Bengal: conch shells at Sealdah, a Tagore song in Diamond Harbour, chants of "We want justice" and "The night is ours". A poster of a red hand holding a crescent moon went viral. Men came in solidarity. Party flags were banned, while flags of marginalised groups, including LGBTQ+ communities, were welcome.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a>,<a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The same night showed how exposed a leaderless crowd can be. At around 12:40 am, about 40 people posing as demonstrators stormed the hospital and damaged property, police said.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

### The doctors hold the line

The junior doctors became the spine of what came next. On 20 August, the Supreme Court, which had taken up the case on its own, set up a ten-member National Task Force on the safety of medical professionals. "The nation cannot await a rape or murder for real changes on the ground," the Court said, and it warned West Bengal not to use state power against peaceful protesters.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The doctors brought evidence. An Indian Medical Association survey of 3,885 doctors, most of them women and under 35, found that about a third felt unsafe or very unsafe on night shifts, and 45% had no duty room at night.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

### Not concessions

The state's first answer included advice that women doctors avoid night shifts and shifts longer than 12 hours. On 17 September the Supreme Court objected, and Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud put it in six words:<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

> "Women need equal opportunities, not concessions."

The state agreed to drop the clauses. It told the Court it had sanctioned 724 duty rooms for women doctors and 563 for men, and would install 415 CCTV cameras and build 82 toilets.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

Kolkata's police commissioner was replaced and two senior health officials were removed. After 41 days, the junior doctors returned to emergency and essential services on 21 September.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup> The street kept going in other forms, too: vigils, rickshaw pullers' marches and neighbourhood street plays, some shown in the photographs below.

### Where it stands

In January 2025, a Sealdah sessions court sentenced the man convicted of the crime to life imprisonment and ordered the state to pay her family ₹17 lakh.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup> The West Bengal assembly's Aparajita Bill, passed on 3 September 2024 to seek the death penalty where a rape victim dies, was reportedly returned by the President in July 2025.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-8" aria-label="Source 8">8</a></sup> The duty rooms and cameras promised to the Court are the part the public can still check.

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Reclaim the Night is the closest recent Indian template for what this generation can build: women-led, non-party, massive, at night, on purpose. Within five days it turned the oldest advice given to women, stay home after dark, upside down.

It also shows the work that has to follow a great night. Crowds can be infiltrated. Anger can be steered towards hanging laws that make headlines but do not build a single duty room. The people who turned outrage into specific, checkable demands were the junior doctors, with surveys, lists and patience.

So march, and bring the receipts. The demand is not a curfew and not a death sentence. It is a safe workplace at 3 am: a lock on the duty-room door, lights, transport home and a complaints committee that works. Safety is a right, not a curfew.
