*Content note: this case study discusses rape, murder and police force against protesters.*

## Context

On the night of 16 December 2012, Jyoti Singh, a 22-year-old physiotherapy intern, got on a private bus in south Delhi with a friend. The men on the bus beat her friend, and raped and tortured her. She died of her injuries on 29 December, in a hospital in Singapore.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

Indian law does not allow the press to name a survivor of sexual violence, so for years the country called her *Nirbhaya*, "fearless". Then, at a vigil in 2015, her mother Asha Devi said it out loud:

> "My daughter's name was Jyoti Singh and I am not ashamed to name her."<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

We use her name because her family asked the world to.

The attack was horrifying. Violence like it was not new. What was new was the answer: young people decided that this time the story would not end with a news cycle and a shrug.

## The story

### The hill

On 21 December, students walked to India Gate and up Raisina Hill, the slope between North and South Block that runs to Rashtrapati Bhavan. It is the most guarded road in the country. They came in jeans and winter jumpers, carrying felt-tip placards that said what everyone was thinking: *We want justice. Stop staring at me.*

The state answered with water cannon, tear gas and batons. Seven metro stations were shut on 22 December, nine on the 24th, and orders banned groups of more than five near the President's house. People came anyway.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

When Jyoti died, the grief went national. Vigils lit up Kolkata, Bengaluru, Chennai, Mumbai, Hyderabad, Kochi, Bhubaneswar and Visakhapatnam: candles, black clothes, black cloth tied over mouths.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

### The committee

On 23 December, the government handed the problem to three people: Justice J. S. Verma, a former Chief Justice of India; Justice Leila Seth, a former High Court judge; and Gopal Subramanium, a former Solicitor General. Deadline: one month.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

They threw the doors open. About 80,000 suggestions arrived, from women's groups, students, lawyers and people abroad.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> A month to the day after they were appointed, on 23 January 2013, they handed over a 631-page report.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup><sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

It did not say what the angriest TV debates wanted. It said the root cause was a failure of governance and policing. It said no to the death penalty for rape. It said marriage cannot mean permanent consent, so the marital-rape exception had to go. It said soldiers accused of sexual offences should not be shielded by the need for government permission to prosecute. And it asked for police reform and an end to the "two-finger test".<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

### The law

Then things moved, fast. A presidential ordinance on 3 February picked up much of the report, and women's groups protested what it left out.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup> Parliament passed the Criminal Law (Amendment) Bill on 19 March (Lok Sabha) and 21 March (Rajya Sabha).<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Stalking, voyeurism, acid attack and sexual harassment became named crimes. Rape was defined more broadly. Not fighting back no longer counted as consent, and a survivor's "character" became irrelevant in court.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup>

Ninety-five days, from a bus in Munirka to a vote in Parliament. That is the protest-to-policy pipeline in its clearest Indian form.

### The unfinished part

The marital-rape exception survived 2013. It survived again in July 2024, when the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaced the old penal code: section 63 still says sex by a man with his wife, if she is 18 or over, is not rape. The Supreme Court has listed challenges to it for final hearing on 18 November 2026.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup>

And the street kept the memory. In August 2024, after a trainee doctor was raped and murdered at RG Kar Medical College in Kolkata, women across Bengal marched at midnight under one call: *Raat Dakhal Karo*, reclaim the night.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Delhi 2012 proves the thing every organiser needs to believe: public anger can become law, and fast, when someone turns it into evidence and drafts.

It also shows the catch. The committee’s boldest ideas (consent inside marriage, accountability for the armed forces, no quick-fix executions) were the easiest for power to drop. Crowds move on faster than laws do.

So here is the job. Keep the receipts. Keep a public tracker of every Verma recommendation: adopted, diluted or ignored. Publish plain explainers in English and Hindi before the 18 November hearing. And hold on to the frame those students carried up Raisina Hill: safety is a right, not a curfew.
