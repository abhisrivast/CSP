*Content note: this case study discusses rape, child marriage and a justice system that failed a survivor. Bhanwari Devi has spoken publicly about her case for more than thirty years, and we use her name as she does.*

## Context

In the 1980s, Rajasthan's government ran a Women's Development Programme that paid village women to be *sathins*, "companions": local workers who carried information about health, rights and the law from door to door. Bhanwari Devi, from Bhateri village, joined in 1985 for ₹200 a month.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

Part of the job was speaking up against child marriage, which was already illegal. In 1992 she tried to stop the wedding of a nine-month-old girl in her village. The wedding went ahead anyway.

Then, at dusk on 22 September 1992, she reported that five men had raped her in her family's bajra field.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> She was a government worker, attacked, she said, for doing her government job.

India had no law against sexual harassment at work. Nobody had written down who is responsible when a woman is attacked for doing her job. Her case forced that question all the way to the Supreme Court, and the answer now covers every workplace in the country.

## The story

### Every step, a wall

The system she worked for failed her almost immediately. The nearby government hospital did not examine her because no woman doctor was there. A larger hospital in Jaipur refused without a magistrate's letter, so she spent the night at a women's police station. The examination finally happened on 24 September, two days after the assault.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

The case went to the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) in January 1993. On 15 November 1995, a Jaipur sessions court acquitted all five men of gang rape; two were convicted of lesser offences. The state appealed on 18 January 1996, but only after women's groups marched, wrote newspaper columns and held meetings with officials.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

### From one woman to every workplace

Women's organisations, the first of them a group called Vishaka, had already gone to the Supreme Court with a bigger argument. When a woman is attacked for doing her job, they said, the harm is not only a crime against her. It breaks her fundamental rights to equality, to work and to dignity, and the state has to set rules that prevent it.

On 13 August 1997, a bench of Chief Justice J. S. Verma and Justices Sujata Manohar and B. N. Kirpal agreed. Drawing on CEDAW, the UN women's rights treaty India ratified in 1993, the Court wrote guidelines that would bind every employer "until suitable legislation is enacted". Each workplace would need a complaints committee headed by a woman, with at least half its members women and an outsider, such as an NGO, on board.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

### Sixteen more years

The law took sixteen more years. In January 2013, a committee chaired by the same J. S. Verma, now retired, read the pending Bill and pushed back: domestic workers had to be covered, the required "conciliation" step had to go, and a committee inside the employer's own organisation "defeats the purpose".<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup>

The President signed the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, known as the POSH Act, on 22 April 2013, and it came into force on 9 December 2013. It covers domestic workers, trainees and volunteers. Every employer must set up an Internal Committee; every district must run a Local Committee for workplaces with fewer than ten workers, or when the complaint is against the boss. A woman has three months to complain, extendable by three more, and the inquiry must finish within 90 days.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### Rules on paper

Rules need people to run them. Right to Information (RTI) requests sent to 655 districts in 2016–17 found that only 29% said they had a Local Committee; 56% did not answer at all.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup> In May 2023 the Supreme Court found "serious lapses" a decade after the Act, and ordered governments to check every office.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup> In December 2024 it went further: a named officer and a Local Committee in every district, and nodal officers listed online.<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

### Still waiting

And Bhanwari Devi? When ThePrint visited in September 2023, her appeal was still pending in the Rajasthan High Court, and four of the five accused had died. She had retired as a sathin in 2021, on ₹3,000 a month. Her verdict on the system, three decades on, was short:<sup style="line-height:0"><a href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

> "They are not believing the daughters even today."

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Every office, college, hospital and start-up in India that has a harassment committee owes it to a village worker who refused to stay quiet. That is the kind of story this generation needs to know by heart: one woman, a few organised groups and a sharp legal argument, rewriting the rules for millions.

It is also a warning. A court can hand down a right in a day, and a system can leave it on paper for decades. The win is where the work starts.

So the job now is implementation. Know the law. Find your committee. File the RTI. Publish what you find, in plain words, for the group chat. And never let the precedent become more famous than the person: "Vishaka" is a word every HR department knows, while Bhanwari Devi's own appeal was still waiting when reporters last checked.
