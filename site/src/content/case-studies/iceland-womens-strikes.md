## Context

In the early 1970s, Icelandic women who worked outside the home earned less than 60% of what men earned. Only three of the 60 members of the Althing, Iceland's parliament, were women: about 5%.

The United Nations declared 1975 International Women's Year. In Iceland, a member of the Redstockings, a radical women's group, proposed a strike to mark it. Other women's organisations worried that a strike would frighten people off, and that women could be sacked for joining one. So the coalition chose a gentler name: Kvennafrídagurinn, the Women's Day Off.

That choice mattered. A day off was something a farmer's wife, a factory worker, a teacher and a grandmother could all say yes to. The plan was simple and total: on 24 October, women would stop paid work, housework and childcare, and see what the country looked like without them.

## The story

On the morning of 24 October 1975, most of Iceland's women simply did not turn up. More than 90% took part, according to Iceland's foreign ministry. They stopped paid work, and they also stopped cooking, cleaning and looking after children. Schools, phone services and newspaper printing were disrupted.

In the afternoon, around 25,000 women gathered in central Reykjavík. In a country of about 220,000, that was more than one in ten of the whole population in one place, on one afternoon, saying one thing: look who keeps this country running.

### What changed next

Within a year, parliament passed a law guaranteeing women and men equal rights, and a Gender Equality Council was set up. In 1980, Vigdís Finnbogadóttir was elected president: the world's first democratically elected woman president. She later said the Day Off was the first step.

The strike did not stay a one-off. Women walked out again in 1985, and then on later anniversaries in 2005, 2010, 2016 and 2018. From 2005 the walkouts gained a sharp new idea. Campaigners worked out the minute in the working day after which women were, on average, no longer paid compared with men, and left work at exactly that time: 14:08 in 2005, 14:25 in 2010, 14:38 in 2016.

A statistic had become a clock. Anyone could understand it, anyone could act on it, and the next walkout time showed at a glance whether things had moved.

### Proof, not promises

On 1 January 2018, a new law took effect requiring employers with 25 or more staff to get their pay systems certified as equal, every year. The idea flips the usual burden: instead of waiting for a worker to complain, employers have to show, every year, that their pay system is fair.

### The full day returns

On 24 October 2023, for the first time since 1975, women and non-binary people were asked to stop paid and unpaid work for a whole day. Prime Minister Katrín Jakobsdóttir joined them. Thousands gathered on Arnarhóll hill in Reykjavík.

For the 50th anniversary in 2025, a coalition of more than 50 organisations, including the main union federations, the national queer organisation and UN Women Iceland, called another full-day strike for women and non-binary people. It was a personal choice, not a strike under labour law. And this time it came with a deadline. The coalition gave the government until 24 October 2025 to turn its demands into law: pay transparency, a revaluation of women's jobs, preschool places after parental leave, training for judges and police on gender-based violence, and action on misogynist hate speech.

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Iceland shows that a movement does not need anger to shock a culture. It needs everyone to notice the same thing at the same time.

India has its own clock waiting to be set. Women who do unpaid domestic work spend 289 minutes a day on it; men who do any spend 88, according to the government's Time Use Survey 2024. That gap is invisible because it happens at home, one kitchen at a time. Iceland made a similar gap visible by giving it a time, a date and a public square. A Fourth Wave movement can do the same: turn one hard number into a ritual people can join from their phone, their office or their street, and come back to it every year until the number moves.

The other lesson is patience. Iceland has topped the World Economic Forum's gender gap ranking for 17 years running, and its women are still striking. Winning is not a day. It is a calendar.
