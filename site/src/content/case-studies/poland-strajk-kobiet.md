*Content note: this case study mentions deaths linked to restrictions on abortion care, and the prosecution of protesters.*

## Context

Poland already had one of Europe's strictest abortion laws. Since 1993, abortion had been legal only if the pregnancy put the woman's life or health at risk, came from a crime such as rape or incest, or involved severe foetal impairment.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> Even then, legal abortions were rare: 1,110 were recorded in 2019, almost all of them on the foetal-impairment ground.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

In 2016, an anti-abortion citizens' initiative, the "Stop Aborcji" (Stop Abortion) bill, wanted to go further: it would have banned almost all abortions, with up to five years in prison for women and for doctors.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

The governing party had a majority in parliament, and few expected women to win. What happened next became a template other movements have copied ever since: Polish women stopped working. This is the story of what happened when they did.

## The story

### Black Monday, 2016

It started with clothes. On 22 September 2016, people across Poland posted selfies dressed in black under the hashtag #czarnyprotest, "black protest". Then the actor Krystyna Janda suggested on Facebook something bigger: a strike, modelled on the day in 1975 when Iceland's women stopped all paid and unpaid work.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

On Monday 3 October, women stayed away from work, school and housework. It rained all day. Crowds in Warsaw, Gdańsk, Łódź, Wrocław and Kraków turned into seas of black umbrellas, and the umbrella became the symbol: shelter for women's rights.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup> Up to 100,000 people protested across the country.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

Three days later, the Sejm threw out the Stop Aborcji bill by 352 votes to 58.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-1" aria-label="Source 1">1</a></sup>

### The court, 2020

The next attempt came through a court. On 22 October 2020, the Constitutional Tribunal ruled 11–2 that abortion for foetal impairment was unconstitutional. That one ground had covered 1,074 of the 1,110 legal abortions recorded in 2019: about 97%.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

Protests started that night. The red lightning bolt of the All-Poland Women's Strike, *Strajk Kobiet*, went up on windows, masks and cardboard. By 26 October protests had reached 150 towns and cities.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> On 28 October came a national strike under the line "I'm not going to work": police counted about 430,000 people at 410 protests, during a pandemic.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> Two days later, around 100,000 marched in Warsaw.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

The demands grew with the crowds: an independent judiciary, sex education, contraception, an end to state funding for religious teaching in schools.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup> The state's answer was the prosecutor. Organisers of "illegal" gatherings, the national prosecutor told his offices, should be investigated for causing an epidemiological threat, an offence carrying up to eight years.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-4" aria-label="Source 4">4</a></sup> Leaders of the Women's Strike were later charged.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

On 27 January 2021, the ruling was published and took effect. The streets filled again that evening.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-3" aria-label="Source 3">3</a></sup>

### The ballot box, 2023

No one can prove that a march became a vote. But on 15 October 2023, turnout hit a record 74%, and among 18–29-year-olds it jumped to 68.8%, from 46.4% four years earlier.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-5" aria-label="Source 5">5</a></sup> A record 136 women were elected to the Sejm, 29.6% of its members.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-6" aria-label="Source 6">6</a></sup> A new coalition government took office.

### The catch

A new government did not mean a new law. On 12 July 2024, the Sejm rejected, 218 to 215, a bill to decriminalise helping someone get an abortion up to 12 weeks; almost all of one coalition partner voted against it. Bills to allow abortion on request stayed in committee.<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-7" aria-label="Source 7">7</a></sup>

Meanwhile, the cost kept rising. Since the ruling, several women have died of septic shock after doctors delayed treatment, and more than 70 protests followed under the slogan "Not one more".<sup><a class="fn-ref" href="#src-2" aria-label="Source 2">2</a></sup>

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Poland shows both halves of the toolkit. A ready-made format (wear black, strike on a Monday, bring an umbrella) brought a country out in days and defeated a bill. A symbol simple enough to draw on a mask filled 150 towns in 2020.

It also shows what comes after. A court can undo a street win overnight. An election can change a government without changing a law. Movements have to work at three levels at once: the street, the ballot box and the bill, and keep publishing the human cost while they do.

For CSP the takeaways are practical. Agree a rapid-response format before you need it. Register first-time voters and explain why their vote counts. And organise in small towns, not only in the capital.
