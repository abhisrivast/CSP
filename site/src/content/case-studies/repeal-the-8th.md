*Content note: this page discusses abortion and the death of a woman in pregnancy.*

## Context

In 1983 Ireland’s voters added the Eighth Amendment to the Constitution. Article 40.3.3° gave “the unborn” an equal right to life with the mother, and made abortion almost impossible to obtain legally in Ireland.

The ban was tested again and again. In 1992 the Supreme Court ruled in the X case that abortion was permitted where a pregnancy threatened a woman’s life, including through the risk of suicide. Yet for two decades no law set out how that would work. In 2010 the European Court of Human Rights ruled against Ireland in *A, B and C v Ireland*. Many women travelled abroad, often in secret, for care they could not get at home.

In 2012, Savita Halappanavar died in hospital after a miscarriage, having been refused an abortion. Her death became a turning point. The government responded with a narrow law, the Protection of Life During Pregnancy Act 2013. Campaigners decided that only repealing the amendment itself would do.

## The story

On 17 November 2012, thousands rallied in Dublin for Savita. Over the next years, a grassroots campaign grew under a simple name: Repeal the 8th. Black “REPEAL” jumpers became a familiar sight on Irish streets.

### The assembly

The government chose an unusual route. In October 2016 it convened a Citizens’ Assembly: about 100 people chosen at random, with no politicians, chaired by the Supreme Court judge Mary Laffoy. They heard from an expert advisory group and 17 advocacy groups, and received more than 12,000 submissions from the public.

Then they voted. In its report of 29 June 2017, 79 of the 91 voting members (87%) said Article 40.3.3° should not be kept in full, and 52 (64%) recommended that termination without restriction as to reasons should be lawful. Ordinary people, given time and evidence, had reached a clear view, and their report gave lawmakers a clear signal.

### The campaign

The referendum was set for 25 May 2018. The Together for Yes coalition brought together the Abortion Rights Campaign, the Coalition to Repeal the Eighth and the National Women’s Council, with unions, students, civil-liberties groups and disability organisations. Volunteers knocked on doors across the country. Women told their own stories of travelling abroad. Strike 4 Repeal borrowed its tactics from Poland’s Black Protest. Five days before the vote, Savita’s parents publicly called for a Yes.

Irish citizens abroad cannot generally vote by post, so thousands travelled home to vote, sharing their journeys under #HomeToVote.

### The result

On 25 May 2018, 66.40% voted Yes on a turnout of 64.13%; 2,159,655 votes were cast, the most in any Irish constitutional referendum to that date. The Yes side won by 706,349 votes. All four regions voted Yes, from 57.5% in Connacht-Ulster to 75.5% in Dublin; Donegal was the only constituency to vote No. Exit polls by The Irish Times and RTÉ found that every age group voted Yes except those aged 65 and over, and that the youngest voters were the most strongly in favour.

President Higgins signed the amendment into law on 18 September 2018. A new law followed in December, and services began in January 2019. In 2024, 10,852 terminations were notified under it, according to the Department of Health.

## Why it matters for the Fourth Wave

Repeal shows that a hard question can be settled by more democracy, not less. A randomly chosen group of citizens, given evidence and time, found common ground that politicians had avoided for 35 years. Then a coalition turned that report into a vote, and young people turned the vote into a landslide.

A Fourth Wave movement in India can borrow the method. India’s Constitution already recognises the village assembly, the gram sabha (Article 243A). A CSP people’s assembly on one question, with random selection, open evidence and published submissions, would be a powerful way to show legislators what ordinary people decide when they are trusted with the facts.

The ending matters too. Winning the vote was half the job; access is still uneven. Count the services, publish the gaps and keep going.
