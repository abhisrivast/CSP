The Fourth Wave of Feminism is the feminism that grew up on phones and on the street from about 2012: campaigns against sexual violence, harassment and online abuse, powered by hashtags, testimony and mass protest, and shaped by an intersectional lens. It has no founder and no official start date; it is a name journalists and activists gave to something that was already happening. This guide explains where the name comes from, what is new about it, why some people question the whole "wave" idea, and where India fits.

## Where does the "wave" idea come from?

Numbering feminist history in waves caught on after a 1968 *New York Times Magazine* article by Martha Weinman Lear called "The second feminist wave". Looking back, the first wave became the long fight for the vote and for basic legal rights. The second was the women's liberation movement of the late 1960s and 1970s. The third, from the 1990s, argued about identity and culture. The fourth is the one most people reading this have lived through.

Treat the table as a map, not a law of nature. The labels come from the US and UK, and India's story does not fit them neatly. In January 1848, six months before the Seneca Falls convention that usually opens the Western "first wave" story, Savitribai Phule, Jyotirao Phule and Sagunabai Kshirsagar opened one of the first girls' schools started by Indians, at Bhide Wada in Pune. Our [public memory timeline](/memory/) starts there for a reason.

## When did the Fourth Wave start?

Most accounts point to about 2012. The US writer Jennifer Baumgardner placed the start nearer 2008, when a new, online generation of activists was already organising. In 2013 the British journalist Kira Cochrane, in her book *All the Rebel Women*, described the new wave as defined by technology: phones and platforms let women build fast, popular campaigns without waiting for permission.

## What is actually new about it?

Plenty of fourth-wave demands are old. What changed is the toolkit, and who holds it.

1. **The phone is both megaphone and battleground.** The same platforms that spread #MeToo also host deepfakes, doxxing and the manosphere.
2. **Testimony at scale.** One story is easy to dismiss. Thousands of stories, collected in one place, become evidence.
3. **Intersectionality as the starting point.** The idea, named by Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989, that gender combines with caste, class, race, disability and sexuality is now expected, even where practice falls short. Our [explainer on intersectional feminism](/resources/intersectional-feminism-explained/) goes deeper.
4. **Leadership from the Global South.** Delhi, Buenos Aires and Kolkata set the pace as often as London or New York.
5. **It moves in bursts.** The scholar Prudence Chamberlain calls this "affective temporality": shared outrage brings huge numbers out at once, then the energy ebbs.

## Why do some people question the "wave" idea?

Good movements argue with themselves, and this is a useful argument.

- **It makes history look tidy.** The philosopher Linda Nicholson argued in 2010 that the metaphor had outlived its usefulness because it suggests one feminism that rises and falls. She suggested a kaleidoscope instead: many patterns, all moving at once.
- **It hides the people who never stopped.** The historians in *No Permanent Waves* (2010) showed that women of colour and working-class women kept organising in the so-called quiet years between waves.
- **It is centred on the Global North.** The standard story starts in the US and UK and treats everyone else as a late arrival.
- **It sets generations against each other.** Every wave defines itself against the last. Nicola Rivers argues for an intergenerational feminism instead.
- **It has fourth-wave blind spots of its own.** Critics point to slacktivism, to women who are offline because of cost or disability, to brands selling empowerment, and to call-out culture that can split allies over small disagreements.

We agree with much of this. Cultural Shock Party uses "Fourth Wave" as a banner, not a box: a promise about what we will build, while being honest that it is one strand of a much older, plural story.

## What does the Fourth Wave look like in India?

India did not import the Fourth Wave. It helped make it.

The roots run deep. Tarabai Shinde's Marathi pamphlet *Stri Purush Tulana* (1882), which turned the blame men heaped on women back on men, is one of the earliest modern feminist texts in India. Read more in our guide to [Indian feminists your textbooks skipped](/resources/indian-feminists-history-forgot/).

The modern moment arrived in December 2012. Students who filled Raisina Hill after the Delhi bus attack pushed the government into setting up the Justice Verma Committee, which reported in a month; the Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013 added offences such as stalking, voyeurism and acid attack. One recommendation was not adopted: removing the exception that stops marital rape from counting as rape. It survives today as Exception 2 to section 63 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita. The full story is in our case study, [Delhi 2012: from protest to the Justice Verma Committee](/case-studies/delhi-2012-justice-verma/).

Since then, the pattern keeps repeating: campus campaigns like [Pinjra Tod](/case-studies/pinjra-tod/), #MeTooIndia in newsrooms and film sets, and the midnight marches of [Reclaim the Night in Bengal](/case-studies/reclaim-the-night-bengal-2024/), which began with a single social-media call by Rimjhim Sinha. Each one claimed public space, at night, at work or online, as women's space too.

The unfinished business is power. Parliament voted for one-third reservation in 2023; three years on, it is law but not yet reality. Our explainer on [the Women's Reservation Act](/resources/womens-reservation-act-explained/) tracks what passed, what stalled and what comes next.

## What does the Fourth Wave need next?

<Placard text="Every generation remixes the last" color="pink" tilt={-4} size="m" decorative />

Hashtags proved that millions of people care. They did not, on their own, build anything that lasts. We think the next stretch of the Fourth Wave needs infrastructure, the boring word for the things that keep a movement alive after the trend dies:

1. **Ideas.** A clear, argued answer to "what are we actually asking for?", which is what our [manifesto](/manifesto/) tries to do, in public, for consultation.
2. **Research.** Receipts that test those ideas against real lives and real institutions, so the case is hard to dismiss.
3. **Memory.** A record of the struggles that came before, so nobody starts from zero and every new campaign can remix the last.
4. **Organised action.** Every online campaign paired with something offline and a specific ask of an institution, from a college to Parliament.

And it needs everyone. The manosphere is selling loneliness to boys; we would rather offer them friendship and a part in this. Our [guide for allies on boys, men and the manosphere](/resources/boys-men-and-the-manosphere/) explains how.
