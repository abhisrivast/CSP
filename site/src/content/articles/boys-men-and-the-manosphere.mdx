The manosphere is a loose online network of influencers, podcasts, forums and short videos that sells boys and young men a narrow idea of manhood and a grudge against women. It is an industry, not a conspiracy: the products are attention, courses and merchandise, and the market is loneliness. This guide is for anyone who cares about a boy, whether you are his parent, teacher, sister or best friend: what is going on, what the data says, and what you can actually say.

## What is the manosphere?

It started as a handful of forums. Today it mostly lives in your feed: confident men in short clips telling boys how to get rich, get fit, get respect and "handle" women. Some of the advice is harmless, even useful. Mixed in with it is a darker message: women are liars or prizes, feelings are weakness, and real men dominate.

We are careful with one point. **The problem is the industry, not boys.** A 14-year-old who watches a gym influencer is not a misogynist; he is a 14-year-old looking for answers. Our job is to offer him better ones. (If you need the words, our glossary has [manosphere](/resources/glossary/#manosphere), [misogyny](/resources/glossary/#misogyny) and [allyship](/resources/glossary/#allyship).)

## How many boys does it reach?

More than most adults realise. The best recent survey comes from the men's health charity Movember, which asked more than 3,000 young men aged 16 to 25 in the UK, the US and Australia about their online lives.

Read the last receipt the other way round, too: 69% of Gen Z men do **not** agree. Most boys are not lost to this. They are the majority we can talk to.

## Why does it pull boys in?

Because it answers real questions with fake certainty.

1. **Loneliness.** Many boys grow up with fewer places to talk about feelings. In the 2026 Ipsos survey, 30% of Gen Z men said men should not say "I love you" to their friends. An influencer who speaks to you every night can feel like a friend.
2. **Pressure to be tough.** 43% of Gen Z men in the same survey said young men should try to be physically tough even if they are not naturally big. Gym and "discipline" content is the gateway.
3. **Real worries.** Exams, jobs, money and dating are genuinely stressful. A video that says "it's women's fault, and here's my course" offers a villain and a plan in sixty seconds.
4. **Feeds that learn.** Platforms show you more of whatever keeps you watching. A curious click can become a steady diet.

In Movember's survey, 43% of the young men who watched these influencers said they found them motivating. That is the honest starting point: the content gives boys something. We have to give them something better.

## What was the "Adolescence" moment?

In March 2025, Netflix released *Adolescence*, a drama about a 13-year-old boy whose online world has been shaped by manosphere ideas. It drew 96.7 million views in its first three weeks. In the UK the Prime Minister backed showing it in schools, it was made free for secondary schools, and a parliamentary committee opened an inquiry into misogyny, the manosphere and online content.

The show mattered because it gave families a shared reference point. Parents who had never heard the word "manosphere" suddenly had a way in: "Have you seen it? What did you think?" If you watch it with a teenager, check the age rating first, and treat it as the start of a conversation, not a lecture.

## What does it look like in India?

The same videos travel here, in English, Hindi and every other language, mostly on YouTube and Instagram Reels. In late 2025, DataReportal put YouTube's advertising reach in India at about 50 crore (500 million) and Instagram's at about 48 crore (481 million).

Indian attitudes give mixed signals, and that is good news for anyone who wants to talk. In Ipsos's 2026 survey of urban, online Indians, 65% of men called themselves feminists, yet 52% of all respondents agreed that a wife should always obey her husband. Many boys already like the idea of equality. They need help living it.

And girls want boys in the room. In Girlguiding's 2025 survey of girls in the UK, 76% of those aged 11 to 16 wanted more discussion with boys at school about the impact of sexism. Schools here can learn from that.

## What actually helps, long term?

1. **Get curious, not furious.** Shaming a boy for what he watches usually teaches him to hide it. Ask, listen, then ask again.
2. **Separate the boy from the content.** "That video says something cruel" is different from "you are cruel".
3. **Name the business model.** Once a boy sees that the anger is the product, it loses some of its shine.
4. **Offer something better.** Real friends, sport, music, mentors, and men in his life who cook, care and say "I love you" out loud.
5. **Keep girls safe too.** If content turns into threats, harassment or sharing someone's photos, take it seriously. Intimate images shared without consent can be reported at cybercrime.gov.in or on 1930.

## What you can do

1. **Watch one video he likes, with him.** Then ask what he thought. You will learn more in ten minutes than in a month of guessing.
2. **Send him the myths.** Share [myths 05 and 06](/resources/feminism-myths-and-receipts/) with a brother, friend or student, without a lecture attached.
3. **Ask the school.** Find out whether boys get time to talk about online misogyny, consent and friendship, and offer to help make it happen.
4. **Model it at home.** Boys copy what men do. Share the cooking and caring, and say why; our data story [Care is work](/resources/care-is-work/) has the numbers.
5. **Sign up to the invitation.** Position 10 of [our manifesto](/manifesto/) says boys and men are invited. If you are 18 or over, [join us](/join/) and bring a friend. Yes, boys too.
