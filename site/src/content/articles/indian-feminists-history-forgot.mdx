faq:
  - q: "Who was India’s first woman teacher?"
    a: "Savitribai Phule is widely remembered as India’s first woman teacher. In January 1848 she, Jyotirao Phule and Sagunabai Kshirsagar opened a girls’ school at Bhide Wada in Pune, one of the first girls’ schools started by Indians."
  - q: "What is one of the earliest feminist texts written in India?"
    a: "Tarabai Shinde’s Marathi pamphlet Stri Purush Tulana (A Comparison of Women and Men), published in 1882, is one of the earliest modern feminist texts in India. It argued that men had every fault they blamed on women, and it was largely forgotten until it was republished in 1975."
  - q: "How many women helped write India’s Constitution?"
    a: "Fifteen women sat in the Constituent Assembly, which debated the Constitution from December 1946 to January 1950. They included Hansa Mehta and Dakshayani Velayudhan, the Assembly’s only Dalit woman member."
  - q: "Who was Bhanwari Devi?"
    a: "Bhanwari Devi was a village-level worker (saathin) in Rajasthan’s Women’s Development Programme who reported being gang-raped in 1992 after trying to stop a child marriage. Women’s groups took the issue to the Supreme Court, which on 13 August 1997 laid down the Vishaka guidelines, India’s first binding rules against sexual harassment at work."
  - q: "Who founded SEWA?"
    a: "Ela Bhatt, a lawyer and organiser from Ahmedabad, founded the Self Employed Women’s Association, which was registered as a trade union on 12 April 1972. It organised women in informal work, such as vegetable vendors and waste-recyclers, whom existing unions had left out."

Indian feminism has a long, home-grown history, and these 12 women are a good place to start: Savitribai Phule, Fatima Sheikh, Tarabai Shinde, Pandita Ramabai, Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain, Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay, Aruna Asaf Ali, Dakshayani Velayudhan, Hansa Mehta, Ela Bhatt, Gaura Devi and Bhanwari Devi. Between 1848 and 1997 they opened schools, wrote back to the men who blamed women, helped write the Constitution, organised workers and saved forests. Here is what each of them did, when, and how we know.

This is not a ranking, and it is nowhere near complete. Think of it as twelve doors into a much bigger house.

## Why haven't more of us heard of them?

History is usually told through rulers, wars and a handful of famous names. Many of these women worked in Marathi, Bengali or Malayalam rather than English, in villages and unions rather than palaces, and some came from castes and communities that official histories pushed to the edges. Their writing went out of print. Tarabai Shinde's pamphlet waited 93 years to be republished.

That is why remembering them is political, not nostalgic. Every one of them used a tactic that still works.

## Why does remembering them matter now?

Because nobody should have to start from zero. Each of these women left a tool you can still pick up: a school, a pamphlet, a shelter, a story, a march, a clause, a union, a human chain around a tree, a court case.

Their stories also show something the word "wave" hides: Indian feminism has been intersectional since at least 1848. Caste, religion, class and language shaped every one of these lives. Our explainer on [intersectional feminism, with Indian examples](/resources/intersectional-feminism-explained/) picks up that thread, and [What is the Fourth Wave of Feminism?](/resources/what-is-fourth-wave-feminism/) shows how today's movement grew out of theirs.

## What you can do

1. **Pick one name and share it.** Send one profile from this page to the group chat that needs it, with the source.
2. **Walk the timeline.** Our [public memory timeline](/memory/) runs from 1848 to 2026, and every entry is sourced.
3. **Read them in their own words.** Start with *Sultana's Dream* or *Stri Purush Tulana* from [the Fourth Wave reading (and watching) list](/resources/fourth-wave-reading-list/).
4. **Ask your school or college** whether these women are taught, and suggest one for the next assembly, project or wall.
5. **Make memory part of the manifesto.** Clause 8 of [the Fourth Wave Manifesto](/manifesto/) says memory is power. Read it, then [join us](/join/) if you are 18 or over.
