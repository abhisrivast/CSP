Care is work because someone has to do it every single day, and in India that someone is usually a woman. Among people who do unpaid housework, women spend 289 minutes a day on it and men 88: a gap of more than three hours, every day, according to the government's Time Use Survey 2024. Counting that work, sharing it at home and funding care services is how a country gives women their time back.

## What did the Time Use Survey find?

The Time Use Survey is run by the National Statistics Office in the Ministry of Statistics (MoSPI). Between January and December 2024 it asked every member aged 6 and over in 1,39,487 households what they did in each half-hour of the previous day: 4,54,192 people in all. It is only the second survey of its kind in India, after 2019.

It sorts the day into paid work, [unpaid domestic work](/resources/glossary/#care-work) (cooking, cleaning, washing, shopping, fetching water), unpaid caregiving (children, older people, the sick), learning, leisure and self-care. Here is what it found.

One detail matters when you share these numbers. The minutes are averages **per participant**: they count only the people who did that activity on the survey day. So read "289 minutes" as "women who do housework spend 289 minutes on it". The second receipt shows why that is the polite version: most men did none at all.

## What does the gap add up to?

Averaged across everyone, not just participants, unpaid domestic work takes up 16.4% of a woman's whole day and 1.7% of a man's. Add paid work and caregiving, and our calculation from the same survey gives women about 354 minutes of work a day against men's 324. Women work longer. The difference is that only about a fifth of women's working time is paid, while nearly nine-tenths of men's is.

<Receipt figure="51 days" label="a year: roughly how much more time women who do housework spend on it than men who do, at 201 extra minutes a day" source="CSP calculation from MoSPI Time Use Survey 2024" href="https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2106113" date="Feb 2025" tone="marigold" note="289 minus 88 minutes, times 365 days: about 1,220 hours, or 51 round-the-clock days." />

There is a little good news. Women aged 15 to 59 who do housework spent about 305 minutes a day on it in 2024, down from 315 in 2019, and the share of women in that age group doing paid work rose from 21.8% to 25%. Men's participation in paid work rose too, to 75%. The gap is narrowing, slowly.

## Is this only an Indian problem?

No. It is the same story almost everywhere, at different volumes.

In Asia and the Pacific, 52% of women outside the labour force give care as the reason, according to the same ILO estimates. And the work is enormous. The ILO's 2018 count, from 64 countries, put unpaid care at 16.4 billion hours a day, 76.2% of it done by women. Paid at minimum wage, it would have been worth 9% of global GDP, about US$11 trillion. That is old data, but nobody has produced a smaller number since.

What happens when it stops? Iceland found out. On 24 October 1975, more than 90% of its women took the day off paid work, housework and childcare. Read our case study of [Iceland's women's strikes](/case-studies/iceland-womens-strikes/) to see how one day changed the country's politics.

## What is care infrastructure?

Care infrastructure is everything that shares out the work of care so it doesn't all land on one person, usually a woman, in every home. Think of it like roads or electricity: you don't notice it until it is missing.

1. **Childcare.** Crèches and pre-schools close to home or work, open when people actually work.
2. **Care for older and disabled people.** Day centres, home visits and support for family carers.
3. **Time rules.** Paid maternity leave, paternity leave that men actually take, and flexible hours for anyone who cares for someone.
4. **The basics nearby.** Water, clean cooking fuel and safe transport, so hours aren't lost to fetching and waiting.
5. **Fair pay for care workers.** Nannies, domestic workers, nurses and community health workers do care for money. UN Women says women are 80% of paid domestic workers worldwide, and 90% of them lack social protection.

## Where is it already working?

Care policy is not a dream. Pieces of it already exist, and they are worth copying and improving.

- **India's maternity law.** The Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act of 2017 raised paid maternity leave from 12 weeks to 26 for a woman's first two children. It also told every establishment with 50 or more employees to provide a crèche nearby, which mothers may visit four times a day, and allowed work from home where the job permits. Its limit is obvious: it helps women with formal jobs in bigger workplaces.
- **Bogotá's care blocks.** In October 2020, Colombia's capital launched a District Care System built on three words: recognise, reduce and redistribute care. Its care blocks bring services within a 15 to 20 minute walk of carers' homes, and the system offers carers classes, rest and recreation, and psychological and legal help, according to the city's Secretary for Women.
- **Organising carers and informal workers.** SEWA, registered in Ahmedabad in 1972, showed that women with no boss can still have a union. Read [how SEWA organised India's self-employed women](/case-studies/sewa-self-employed-women/).

## What can households, workplaces and governments do?

### At home

1. **Count it for a week.** Write down who cooks, cleans, shops and cares, and for how long. The list usually settles the argument.
2. **Share the boring jobs, not just the fun ones.** A fair share means the daily, unglamorous work: dishes, laundry, the 6 am tiffin.
3. **Teach it early.** Boys who cook and clean at 10 grow into men who do it at 30. Our guide to [boys, men and the manosphere](/resources/boys-men-and-the-manosphere/) has more on raising boys who care.

### At work

1. **Make leave equal.** Offer paternity leave and expect men to take it.
2. **Fix the crèche.** If your workplace must have one, check that it exists and works. If it doesn't have to, ask for one anyway, or for childcare support.
3. **Flex for everyone.** Flexible hours and remote days for anyone who cares for a child, a parent or a sick partner, not only for mothers.

### In government

1. **Measure it regularly.** Run the Time Use Survey on a fixed schedule and publish the results by state, caste, age and disability.
2. **Build it.** Fund crèches, elder care and water and fuel close to home as core infrastructure, with budgets that can be checked.
3. **Pay care workers fairly.** Decent pay and social protection for domestic workers, nurses and community health workers.
