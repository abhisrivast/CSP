Mostly, yes. In the biggest recent cross-country survey, run by Ipsos and King's College London in 2026, Gen Z women and Gen Z men are each more likely to call themselves feminists than any older generation of women or men. But young women and young men are further apart than any other generation, and a minority of young men hold very traditional views. Here is what the numbers say, what they cannot tell us, and why the hopeful reading is also the accurate one.

## Where do these numbers come from?

Most figures here come from one survey. Ipsos interviewed 23,268 adults in 29 countries between 24 December 2025 and 9 January 2026, with King's College London's Global Institute for Women's Leadership, and published the results for International Women's Day 2026. Interviews were mostly online. In India, about 2,200 people took part, most of them face to face.

Generations are age groups: Gen Z are the youngest adults surveyed, Baby Boomers the oldest. Where we use other surveys, we say so, and we do not stack figures from different years.

## How many young people call themselves feminists?

More than any generation before them, women and men alike.

A majority of Gen Z women (54%) call themselves feminists. Among men, each younger generation is more likely to say so than the one before: from 27% of Baby Boomer men to 36% of Gen Z men. If you have heard that boys are abandoning feminism, these figures say something different.

## So why does everyone talk about a gender divide?

Because the distance between young women and young men is real. On calling yourself a feminist, Gen Z has the widest gap of any generation: 18 percentage points. And on some old ideas about marriage, a minority of young men are more traditional than Baby Boomer men.

Those last two numbers matter. Rigid rules about manhood hurt boys too, and an online industry profits from them. In a 2025 Movember survey of more than 3,000 young men aged 16 to 25 in the UK, US and Australia, 63% watched "men and masculinity" influencers, and 27% of those viewers reported feelings of worthlessness.

**Read the small print.** Ipsos says its Indian sample represents a large part of urban India, socio-economic classes A, B and C in metros and tier 1 to 3 towns, not the country as a whole. The published charts do not break India down by generation.

Older surveys point the same way. Pew's face-to-face survey of 29,999 Indian adults (fieldwork 2019 to 2020) found about nine in ten agreeing that a wife must always obey her husband, while 62% said both parents should share responsibility for childcare. A CSDS-KAS youth survey of 6,122 people aged 15 to 34, published in 2017, found patriarchal views among 57% of young men and 40% of young women.

Put together: equality and the feminist label are popular, and everyday rules at home are changing more slowly. That is why we lead with behaviours and rights, not the label alone.

## Do young Indians even care about politics?

Yes. In Lokniti-CSDS's survey of 1,290 college students aged 18 to 22 (January 2024), 78.7% were very or somewhat interested in politics. It is one student sample, not a national one, and it points to something fixable: interest is high, registration lags.

## Who do young people trust to tell them?

People more than brands.

## What can't this data tell us?

Surveys are useful. They are also blunt. Five limits to keep in mind:

1. **A label is not a behaviour.** Calling yourself a feminist says nothing about who does the dishes.
2. **Samples miss people.** India's figures cover urban India, and in many countries the sample is more urban, educated or affluent than average. Rural and poorer young people, and anyone offline, are under-counted.
3. **There is a gap in the evidence.** We could not find a nationally representative survey from 2024 to 2026 of Indian Gen Z attitudes to gender, broken down by gender.
4. **Surveys do not stack.** Different years, samples and wording give different numbers. Compare like with like.
5. **"Did not agree" is not "disagreed".** Some people choose "neither", and that middle is where minds change.

## Why is the hopeful reading the right one?

Because most people are less traditional than they think everyone else is. Across the 29 countries, Ipsos and King's College London found:

We imagine a more conservative crowd than the one we are actually standing in. Gen Z is the most feminist generation in this survey, for women and for men. The job now is to say out loud what most people already believe.
