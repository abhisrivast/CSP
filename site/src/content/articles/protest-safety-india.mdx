Peaceful protest is a constitutional right in India: Article 19 protects free speech and the right "to assemble peaceably and without arms". To use it safely, plan within the current police rules, never go alone, protect your phone and other people's faces, and know your rights if you are detained.

A good protest feels like a festival with a point: friends, posters, songs and one clear demand. This guide is information, not legal advice; the sources at the end were checked on 24 October 2026.

## What does the law say about protest?

The right is real, and so are the limits. Article 19(3) lets the state place "reasonable restrictions" on assembly in the interests of India's sovereignty and integrity or public order. So check three things before any action:

1. **Permission.** Ask your local police station how to apply, apply in writing, and keep copies of everything.
2. **The place.** Some cities name designated protest sites. On 12 October 2026 ThePrint reported that Jantar Mantar had been de-notified as a Delhi protest venue, with Ramlila Ground, Burari Ground and CBD Ground named instead. **Check the current Delhi Police order before you plan**: rules like this change.
3. **Prohibitory orders.** A magistrate can ban gatherings in an area under section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), which replaced the old Section 144. An order can last up to two months, and Delhi used one at Jantar Mantar in early October 2026.

## What should I carry, and what should I leave at home?

Keep it light enough to walk all day.

1. **Water, a snack and something for the sun or rain.**
2. **A photo ID and a little cash**, in case your phone dies or you need an auto home.
3. **Emergency numbers on paper**: home, your buddy and the free legal aid line, 15100. Phones get lost.
4. **Your medicines**, and a small first-aid kit if you have one.
5. **A sign, a song and a friend.**

Leave behind anything that could look like a weapon (the right is to assemble "without arms"), and valuables you would hate to lose.

## How does the buddy system work?

Pair up before you arrive, and keep your buddy in sight all day. If one of you needs to leave, you both leave.

## How do I keep my phone, and other people's faces, safe?

Every protest is full of cameras, so decide in advance what you are happy to share.

- **Lock your phone** with a PIN, back it up, and turn off location tagging on photos.
- **Ask before you film someone's face.** People have jobs, families and reasons to stay private.
- **Blur or crop faces before you post**, especially anyone who asked not to be shown. Never post a photo of a child without a parent's okay.
- **Don't post live locations** of a women's gathering. Share meeting points privately, with people you know.
- **If someone targets you online**, report it at cybercrime.gov.in or on 1930. Our toolkit [Safe online: deepfakes, doxxing and image-based abuse](/resources/safe-online-toolkit/) covers the rest.

## How do we make it work for everyone?

If a protest only works for fit people with free afternoons, it is not finished. Plan a step-free route with shade, water, toilets and places to sit, and walk at the pace of the slowest person. Put announcements in writing, caption your videos, ask who needs a sign-language interpreter, and publish it all before the day.

Agree a code of conduct, too: no slurs, no harassment, no one left out because of caste, religion, gender or disability.

## I'm under 18. Can I come?

Go with a parent or a trusted adult, never on your own, and make sure someone at home knows where you are. The law protects you differently: a child who is apprehended must be handed to a special juvenile police unit or a child welfare police officer, brought before the Juvenile Justice Board within 24 hours, and never kept in a police lock-up or jail (Juvenile Justice Act 2015, s.10). The Child Helpline is **1098**.

Learning counts as action too: everything in our [guides and explainers](/resources/) is free, with no sign-up.

## What are my rights if the police detain me?

Most protests end without trouble. If yours does not, these rights come from Article 22 of the Constitution and the BNSS, which replaced the CrPC on 1 July 2024. This is information, not legal advice.

1. **Stay calm and do not resist.** If someone forcibly resists arrest, police may use "all means necessary" (BNSS s.43).
2. **Ask why.** You must be told the grounds of your arrest and, if the offence is bailable, that you can be released on bail (Article 22(1); BNSS s.47).
3. **Note who is arresting you.** The officer must wear a clear name tag, and the arrest memo must be attested by a witness and countersigned by you (BNSS s.36).
4. **Have someone told.** The police must inform a relative or friend you name (BNSS s.48).
5. **Ask for a lawyer.** You may meet a lawyer of your choice during questioning (BNSS s.38). Women, children and people in custody can get free legal aid, whatever their income (Legal Services Authorities Act 1987, s.12).
6. **Count the hours.** You must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours (Article 22(2); BNSS s.58).
7. **If you are a woman:** unless circumstances require otherwise, only a woman officer should touch you to make an arrest, and except in exceptional circumstances no woman may be arrested between sunset and sunrise without a magistrate's prior permission (BNSS s.43).

## What should we do afterwards?

After-care is part of the action. Check that everyone is home, then eat, drink water and sleep. If something went wrong, write it down while it is fresh (time, place, names or badge numbers, photos of injuries) and talk to a lawyer before you post about it.

You will not be the first to need help. When at least 5,000 people were detained in Delhi between 10 and 13 October 2026, BehanBox reported that volunteer networks offered legal, medical and transport help. Care is how movements last. If you feel low afterwards, call Tele-MANAS, the government's free mental health helpline, on 14416.
