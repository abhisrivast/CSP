Peaceful protest is a constitutional right in India: Article 19 protects your freedom of speech and your right "to assemble peaceably and without arms". To use that right safely, plan within the current police rules, never go alone, protect your phone and other people's faces, and know your rights if you are detained. This toolkit walks you through it, from a week before to the day after.

A good protest feels like a festival with a point: friends, posters, songs and one clear demand. This guide is information, not legal advice; sources are at the end, checked on 24 October 2026.

## What does the law say about protest?

The right is real, and so are the limits. Article 19(3) lets the state place "reasonable restrictions" on assembly in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India or public order. In practice that means three things to check before any action:

1. **Permission.** Ask your local police station how to apply, and apply in writing, well in advance. Keep a copy of your application and any reply.
2. **The place.** Some cities name designated protest sites. In Delhi, ThePrint reported on 12 October 2026 that Jantar Mantar had been de-notified as a protest venue and that Ramlila Ground, Burari Ground and CBD Ground were named instead. **Check the current Delhi Police order before you plan**: rules like this change.
3. **Prohibitory orders.** A magistrate can ban gatherings in an area under section 163 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS), the successor to the old Section 144. Such an order can last up to two months, and Delhi used one at Jantar Mantar in early October 2026.

## What should I carry, and what should I leave at home?

Keep it light enough to walk all day.

1. **Water, a snack and something for the sun or rain.**
2. **A photo ID and a little cash**, in case your phone dies or you need an auto home.
3. **Emergency numbers on paper**: someone at home, your buddy, and the legal aid helpline, 15100. A phone can be lost, broken or taken.
4. **Your medicines**, and a small first-aid kit if you have one.
5. **A sign, a song and a friend.**

Leave behind anything that could be seen as a weapon (the right is to assemble "without arms"), and valuables you would hate to lose.

## How does the buddy system work?

Pair up before you arrive, and keep your buddy in sight all day. If one of you needs to leave, you both leave.

## How do I keep my phone, and other people's faces, safe?

Every protest is full of cameras. Decide in advance what you are happy to have shared.

- **Lock your phone** with a PIN, back it up, and turn off location tagging on photos.
- **Ask before you film someone's face.** People have jobs, families and reasons to stay private.
- **Blur or crop faces before you post**, especially anyone who asked not to be shown. Never post a photo of a child without a parent's okay.
- **Don't post live locations** of a women's gathering. Share meeting points privately, with people you know.
- **If someone targets you online**, report it at cybercrime.gov.in or on 1930. Our toolkit [Safe online: deepfakes, doxxing and image-based abuse](/resources/safe-online-toolkit/) explains the rest.

## How do we make it work for everyone?

If a protest only works for fit people with free afternoons, it is not finished. Plan a step-free route with shade, water, toilets and places to sit, and walk at the pace of the slowest person. Put announcements in writing, caption your videos, and ask who needs a sign-language interpreter. Publish all of this before the day.

Agree a code of conduct, too: no slurs, no harassment, no one left out because of caste, religion, gender or disability.

## I'm under 18. Can I come?

Go with a parent or a trusted adult, never on your own, and make sure someone at home knows where you are. The law treats you differently, and it protects you: a child who is apprehended must be placed with a special juvenile police unit or a child welfare police officer, produced before the Juvenile Justice Board within 24 hours, and never kept in a police lock-up or jail, as the Supreme Court confirmed in 2020. The Child Helpline is **1098**.

And remember that learning counts. Reading, talking and making posters are action too. Everything in our [resources](/resources/) is free, with no sign-up.

## What are my rights if the police detain me?

Most protests end without trouble. If yours does not, these rights come from Article 22 of the Constitution and the BNSS, which replaced the CrPC on 1 July 2024. This is information, not legal advice.

1. **Stay calm and do not resist.** Police may use "all means necessary" if someone forcibly resists arrest (BNSS s.43).
2. **Ask what is happening, and why.** You must be told the grounds of your arrest, and, if the offence is bailable, that you can be released on bail (Article 22(1); BNSS s.47).
3. **Note who is detaining you.** An officer making an arrest must wear a clear name tag, and the arrest memo must be signed by a witness and by you (BNSS s.36).
4. **Have someone told.** The police must inform a relative or friend you name, and record who they told (BNSS s.48).
5. **Ask for a lawyer.** You may meet a lawyer of your choice during questioning (BNSS s.38). Every woman, every child and every person in custody can get free legal aid, whatever their income (Legal Services Authorities Act 1987, s.12).
6. **Count the hours.** You must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours (Article 22(2); BNSS s.58). If you are detained for not following a police direction, you must be taken before a magistrate or, in petty cases, released within 24 hours (BNSS s.172).
7. **If you are a woman:** unless circumstances require otherwise, only a woman officer should touch you to make an arrest, and save in exceptional circumstances, no woman may be arrested between sunset and sunrise without a magistrate's prior permission (BNSS s.43).
8. **Do not sign anything you do not understand**, and ask for a copy of what you do sign.

## What should we do afterwards?

After-care is part of the action. Check that everyone is home, then eat, drink water and sleep. If something went wrong, write down what happened while it is fresh (time, place, names or badge numbers, photos of injuries) and talk to a lawyer before you post about it.

You will not be the first to need help. When at least 5,000 people were detained in Delhi between 10 and 13 October 2026, BehanBox reported that volunteer networks offered legal, medical and transport help to get them home. Care is how movements last. If you feel low afterwards, call Tele-MANAS, the government's free mental health helpline, on 14416.
