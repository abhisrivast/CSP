Intersectional feminism is feminism that looks at how gender combines with the other parts of who we are, such as caste, class, religion, disability, sexuality and where we live, because those overlaps change what unfairness looks like. The US law professor Kimberlé Crenshaw gave the idea its name in 1989. In India it is much older: when Savitribai Phule opened a school for girls in 1848, she was taking on caste and patriarchy at the same time.

## What does intersectionality mean, in plain words?

Think of a crossroads. Traffic comes from several directions at once, and the person standing in the middle can be hit from any of them. "Intersection" is the old word for that crossroads.

A girl's life is shaped by being a girl. It is also shaped by her caste, her family's money, her religion, whether she has a disability, whether she lives in a village or a city, and who she loves. These do not simply add up, like points on a scorecard. They change each other. Sexism looks different for a Dalit woman cleaning sewers than for an upper-caste woman in a corporate office, even when both are real.

## Where does the word come from?

In 1989 Crenshaw published a paper in the *University of Chicago Legal Forum* with a long title that begins "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex". She looked at court cases brought by Black women in the United States. In one, Black women who sued General Motors argued that the company's hiring shut them out in a way it did not shut out white women or Black men. Courts in cases like this would not let them claim discrimination on the basis of race and sex together, so their real experience fell through the gap between two laws.

That gap is what intersectionality names. Crenshaw went on to co-found the African American Policy Forum, which started the #SayHerName campaign in December 2014 to make sure Black women and girls harmed by police violence were not left out of the story.

## Is intersectionality an Indian idea too?

Yes. India had the idea long before it had the word.

In January 1848, Savitribai Phule, Jyotirao Phule and Sagunabai Kshirsagar started a school for girls at Bhide Wada in Pune, one of the first girls' schools started by Indians. In a society where who was allowed to learn depended on both caste and gender, that was a challenge to both at once. Savitribai's colleague Fatima Sheikh is remembered as having taught alongside her; historians note that the written records about Fatima Sheikh are thin, and her story became a public argument in 2025. More of these foremothers are in [Indian feminists your textbooks skipped](/resources/indian-feminists-history-forgot/) and our [public memory timeline](/memory/).

In the 1990s, Dalit feminists made the argument sharper. In 1995 the scholar Gopal Guru wrote an essay in the *Economic and Political Weekly* called "Dalit Women Talk Differently", arguing that Dalit women had good reasons to speak for themselves, facing patriarchy both from outside and inside their own communities. In 1998 the sociologist Sharmila Rege answered with an essay of the same title, proposing a "Dalit feminist standpoint": start from the lives of Dalit women and the whole picture of caste and gender becomes clearer for everyone. The historian Uma Chakravarti's term [brahmanical patriarchy](/resources/glossary/#brahmanical-patriarchy) describes how caste and the control of women were bound together.

The organising followed the ideas. The National Federation of Dalit Women was formally set up in New Delhi on 11 August 1995, weeks before the UN's world conference on women in Beijing. One of its founders, Ruth Manorama, describes Dalit women as facing discrimination of caste, class and gender at once, and argues that patriarchy cannot be ended without ending caste.

## What does it look like in everyday life?

Here are five people. None of them is a real individual; each one stands for situations that are.

1. **The sanitation worker.** Protesters in Delhi in 2018 demanded an end to manual scavenging, dangerous work that caste still pushes onto some communities. A woman doing that work faces caste stigma, low pay and sexual harassment together, and a campaign about only one of those will not reach her.
2. **The woman in a hijab looking for a flat.** She may be turned away for being a single woman and for being Muslim. Which reason was it? Often both, and neither law nor landlord will say.
3. **The disabled girl in Class 8.** India's disability law says governments must make sure women and children with disabilities enjoy their rights equally with others. That only happens if the school's ramp, the bus and the toilet are planned with her in mind.
4. **The trans woman at a protest.** Trans people in India are still fighting for laws that respect who they are. A feminism that leaves them out is defending a smaller idea of who counts as a woman.
5. **The woman who isn't online.** Many big surveys reach only people with internet access. Ipsos, for example, says its India sample is urban and online, so it does not represent India as a whole. If the data cannot see her, policy cannot either.

## How does Indian law already think intersectionally?

More than people realise. Article 243D of the Constitution reserves at least one-third of panchayat seats for women, and inside the seats reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, at least one-third must go to SC and ST women. The 2023 Women's Reservation Act copies that design for the Lok Sabha and state assemblies, including within SC and ST seats, though it will only start after constituencies are redrawn. Our explainer on [the Women's Reservation Act](/resources/womens-reservation-act-explained/) tracks when.

Proof that designing for the overlap works is sitting in village councils across the country.

## Isn't this just "identity politics"?

No. Intersectionality is not a contest over whose pain is worst, and it is not a way of telling anyone to be quiet. It is a habit of asking one extra question before you act: who will this miss?

Ask it, and campaigns get better. A safe-streets campaign that thinks about women who work night shifts, women in wheelchairs and women who cannot afford autos will design a better city than one built around a student with a smartphone. This is why our [manifesto](/manifesto/) puts it plainly: nobody is free until everybody is.

## What you can do

1. **Ask "who's missing?"** Next time your class, club or group chat plans something, ask who it leaves out: by caste, money, language, disability or distance. Then ask them.
2. **Read Dalit feminists in their own words.** Start with Sharmila Rege's 1998 essay or Ruth Manorama's 2025 piece in Scroll (both in the sources below).
3. **Learn three words.** Look up [intersectionality](/resources/glossary/#intersectionality), [Dalit feminism](/resources/glossary/#dalit-feminism) and [brahmanical patriarchy](/resources/glossary/#brahmanical-patriarchy) in our glossary, then explain one to a friend.
4. **Remember the foremothers.** Share one story from our [public memory timeline](/memory/), like the Phules' school or Bhanwari Devi's fight in our case study on [Bhanwari Devi, Vishaka and the POSH Act](/case-studies/bhanwari-devi-vishaka-posh/).
5. **Put it in writing.** Read manifesto position 3, "Nobody is free until everybody is", and [join us](/join/) if you are 18 or over to help write the next draft.
